## Citation
Lasco, G. (2024). Beyond 'heightism' and 'height premium': An anthropology and sociology of human stature. *Sociology Compass*, 18(2), e13178. https://doi.org/10.1111/soc4.13178 (online Dec 2023)

## Core argument
Height has been studied mainly in economics and psychology as a **premium** (taller people earn more) or a **prejudice** ("heightism"). That framing is Western and individualist. Lasco argues for an anthropology and sociology of stature that treats height as **relational, cultural and materially mediated**. On this view, what height means and what it is worth depends on setting, social relations and the built environment.

## Methods
- A narrative and conceptual literature review building on Feldman's 1975 call for a "sociology of stature".
- No new empirical data.

## Key content
- **The height-premium and heightism literatures** in Western labour markets and politics are summarised as the dominant paradigm.
- **Non-Western counterpoints.** Ethnographic cases (e.g. Buang, Hadza, Datoga) where height carries different or little value.
- **Height as relational.** It is evaluated against others, against gender norms (for example, the expectation that men be taller than their partners), and against the built environment.
- **Height-making practices.** Supplements, surgery and cultural strategies for "becoming tall", plus disability perspectives such as Little People of America.
- **Basketball appears only in passing.** Tall people face expectations of being good at basketball, the fixed 10-ft ring is an example of built-environment standardisation, and media coverage portrays tall female athletes as "grotesque" while short male stars (Messi) are celebrated.

## Critical read
- It is a review and agenda-setting piece, not evidence of height effects in basketball.
- It is useful precisely as the counterweight to "height = ability" reasoning.
