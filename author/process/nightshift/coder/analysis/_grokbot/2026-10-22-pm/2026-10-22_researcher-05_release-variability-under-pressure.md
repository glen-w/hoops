# Free-throw release stability under pressure, and whether fatigue predicts it

**Request:** 2026-10-22-overtime-researcher-05 (paper, high)
**Status:** done. The evidence is partial, and the "predictable from fatigue or workload" part is untested.
**Sources:** OpenAlex, Crossref and Semantic Scholar abstracts. Every DOI below resolved. Zotero keys are in brackets.

## Bottom line (suggestions, not orders)
- **Pressure → free-throw kinematics.** One recent study measures it directly. **Cruz et al. 2026** (*Sci Rep*) found that pressure and sensory occlusion "altered joint kinematics" and raised prefrontal oxygenation (fNIRS) in 24 youth players taking 100 free throws per condition. The abstract doesn't say whether *release variability* specifically went up, so read the full text before quoting a direction. The other direct test, **Mascret et al. 2016**, found **no effect** of a social-stress test on movement execution in novices.
- **What's well established is that miss/make differences show up as late variability.** Mullineaux & Uhl (2010) found elbow–wrist coordination variability rose in the last 0.01 s before release on **misses**. Button et al. (2003) and Verhoeven & Newell (2016) show that skill reduces *joint* variability while release parameters are compensated. Those are the mechanics. Neither is a pressure study.
- **Threat → attention is stronger than threat → kinematics.** Wilson, Vine & Wood (2009) showed anxiety shortened the quiet-eye period and lowered free-throw success. Outside basketball, Causer et al. (2011, shotgun) and Pijpers et al. (2005, climbing) show anxiety changes *movement efficiency and fluency*.
- **Fatigue → release.** The results are mixed and the samples tiny:
  - Uygur et al. (2010) found no change in joint angles after exhaustion (n = 10).
  - Rusdiana et al. (2020) found wrist angular velocity related to outcome under fatigue (n = 10).
  - Erčulj & Supej saw jump height fall with fatigue in one elite player, without a clear accuracy loss.
  - Ardigò et al. (2018) found 3-point accuracy fell 28% at 80% of maximum heart rate.
- **No study found predicts *pressure-induced* variability from a *fatigue or workload baseline*.** That conditional ("does variance rise under threat, and is the rise predictable from fatigue?") is open. The NBA pull (researcher-02) gives an outcome-level look, not a kinematic one, and finds no workload × pressure interaction.

## Where the evidence is thin
- **No in-game NBA release data are public.** Player-tracking release metrics such as Second Spectrum, Noah and ShotTracker are proprietary. Any NBA-level "release variability under pressure" claim would rest on outcome data (FT%), not kinematics.
- Pressure studies use lab manipulations (money, evaluation, video) on youth, novice or collegiate shooters. Their generalisation to NBA clutch moments is an inference.
- Fatigue × pressure *interaction* on kinematics: none found.

## Optional framing
> "In the lab, what fails first under threat is where you look (the quiet eye) and the last hundredth of a second of the elbow–wrist hand-off. Whether a tired body makes that hand-off wobblier when the arena gets loud is still a guess, and the NBA's own numbers (see the FT pull) don't show tired players choking more."
