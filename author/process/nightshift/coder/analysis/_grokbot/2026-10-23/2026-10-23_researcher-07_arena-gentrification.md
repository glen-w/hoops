# Do new arenas gentrify or displace? Barclays, Edmonton, Detroit, Sacramento, and the property-value literature

**Request:** 2026-10-22-researcher-07 · **Status:** partly done (no causal studies exist for Detroit or for displacement itself) · **Prepared by:** Grok Bot nightshift, 23 Oct 2026

## Bottom line (suggestions, not orders)
- **No study I found measures *displacement* (people pushed out) causally for any of the four arenas.** The causal work measures prices, rents, permits and business survival. Displacement and "sport-related gentrification" are documented through interviews and ethnography, mainly in Edmonton.
- **Price effects near arenas are usually positive and local.** Nearby homes and commercial space gain value, fading with distance, often in anticipation before opening: Staples Center (Keeler et al. 2021), Oklahoma City (Chikish et al. 2019), Brooklyn commercial rents (Propheter 2019), and US facilities generally (Feng & Humphreys 2012; Huang & Humphreys 2014). That rising value is the mechanism behind gentrification worries, but it is not proof of displacement.
- **Counterpoint:** when NBA teams *left* Seattle and Charlotte, nearby home prices *rose* 6–14%, so the team itself was a disamenity (Humphreys & Nowak 2017). Sacramento's Golden 1 brought longer police response times and shorter survival for existing nearby shops, and no permit boom (three Propheter papers).
- **Detroit:** only grey literature and a subsidy case study (Sroka 2024). There's a gap here the book can name.

## Where evidence is thin
- Nothing causal measures residents moving out, rent burdens or demographic change around any NBA arena.
- The Barclays causal work covers commercial property only.
- I left the Intuit Dome / Inglewood "Homes Not Domes" fight (Yale L&P Review piece [AQSPEH2K]) as context; there's no outcome study yet.

## What the book can safely say
Studies that compare places near new arenas with places farther away usually find nearby property values going up, often before the doors open. That is the economic engine of gentrification. But no study has yet shown, with a causal design, that an NBA arena pushed residents out. Sacramento's new arena brought shorter lives for existing nearby shops, slower police response on game nights and no building boom. In Edmonton, researchers who interviewed residents describe a "sport-related gentrification" whose costs fell unevenly, especially on the area's Indigenous urban street community and less affluent residents. Price rises are well established; displacement is plausible and documented in qualitative work, but it has not been measured.
