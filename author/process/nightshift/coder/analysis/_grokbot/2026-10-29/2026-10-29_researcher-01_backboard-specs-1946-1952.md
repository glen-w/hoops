# NBA/BAA backboard specifications, 1946–1952: dimensions, materials, construction

## Short answer
- **No BAA or NBA rulebook text with backboard dimensions was found for 1946–52.** The BAA played college-derived rules, so the equipment specs were presumably the National Basketball Committee/NCAA ones. Every dimension below comes from NBC/NCAA sources and is labelled that way. Treat it as the baseline in force, not as verified BAA wording.
- **Baseline board** (1933 **women's** rules text, SBU Archives; equipment specs presumed to match the men's NBC rules of the time [inference]): **6 ft × 4 ft rectangle**, lower edge **9 ft** above the floor, ring **10 ft** high with an **18 in** inside diameter, nearest inside edge of the ring **6 in** from the board face. The board could be plate glass, wood or any other flat rigid material, with a white face. Spectators had to be kept 3 ft behind and to the sides. [LOW–MEDIUM for 1946–52 men's/pro play]
- **Position**: board face **4 ft inside the end line** from 1939-40, up from 2 ft (NCAA Rules History). [HIGH]
- **Shape**: fan-shaped boards legal from **1940-41**. The 1940 NBC experimental standard was **54–60 in wide** and **27–30 in from basket level to the top**, with rounded edges and the basket at the bottom. It weighed "nearly one-half" less and was optional (KU PHOG Allen papers, primary minutes). [HIGH] The final fan dimensions (often given as 35 in high with a 29 in radius) were **not verified**. [GAP]
- **Transparent boards**: authorised by the college rules in **1946-47**, the BAA's first season. [HIGH]
- **League mandate**: on **10 May 1948** the BAA Board of Governors (Hotel Morrison, Chicago) made the **glass backboard mandatory**. The same meeting required approved nets and **orange-painted hoops** and set lighting and floor standards, for "old teams as well as new" (APBR transcription of the BAA minutes). [HIGH] This is the most quotable fact for the book. Separately, the 6 Jun 1946 minutes set a minimum actual floor of 60×100 ft.
- **Glass was already in BAA use in 1946**: on 5 Nov 1946, before the Celtics' first home game at Boston Arena, Chuck Connors broke a **glass** board in warm-ups. ESPN attributes it to a missing **protective rubber pad between the rim and the glass**. A spare glass board was trucked over from Boston Garden. [MEDIUM, journalistic]
- **Material tech**: Pittsburgh Plate Glass pitched **Herculite tempered glass** boards to the NBC in Feb 1940, offering to supply glass only to equipment makers such as Medart (KU PHOG letters). [HIGH] By c.1953 PPG was advertising fan-shaped Herculite boards as "four to five times stronger" than ordinary glass (½ in thickness, OCR garbled). [LOW] Earlier glass: Nurre Mirror Plate Glass (Bloomington) supplied 1.5 in plate-glass boards at IU in 1924 (IU Archives). [MEDIUM] Anything earlier (Purdue c.1916, "permitted 1909") is [unverified].

## Patents (Google Patents, bibliographic data HIGH)
The 16 rows run from the Chicago Gymnasium Equipment glass/wood composite boards (US1736879A, 1929, which records that glass boards were "well liked" but fragile and "likely to fall when broken") to the post-war run of mounting and goal patents. The main makers are **Fred Medart Mfg (St Louis)**, **J.E. Porter (Ottawa IL)** and **Everwear (Springfield OH)**, which made the floor-standing portable backstops that arenas used. The patent most relevant to 1946–52 arenas is **US2553105A (Morey, filed Feb 1947)**: a demountable cantilever frame holding a "wholly transparent rectangular plate" board so spectators can see through it. No patent shows a supplier for a specific BAA arena. No Nurre or Spalding backboard patents turned up.

## What does not exist / was not found

- Any source tying Spalding to backboard manufacture in this period.
- The year the 24×18 in shooter's rectangle was introduced (unknown; later than this period as far as I can tell [unverified]).
