## Core argument
Recreational pickup basketball works as a form of **self-care** for people in high-stress caring professions. It does this through positive physical touch, an affirming and empowering space and social boundary-crossing, all at low cost.

## Methods
- Qualitative ethnography with participant observation at pickup games, plus **10 semi-structured interviews** conducted in June 2018 (5 face-to-face, 5 by phone).
- Purposive sampling of educators and health professionals in East-Coast US metro areas, recruited through Facebook and word of mouth. The sample was balanced on gender, with a wide range of ages and skill levels.
- Thematic analysis. Theoretical framing: self-care literature and the healing-touch and touch literatures from nursing and social work.

## Key findings
- **Positive physical touch.** Contact in pickup (bumping, high-fives, guarding) was felt as restorative rather than threatening.
- **Affirming and empowering space.** Players described confidence and a sense of competence and belonging.
- **Crossing social boundaries.** Play was shared across race, class, profession and age.
- **Affordable and accessible** compared with other self-care options.
- **Recommendations:** more skill-level-specific runs to reduce intimidation, *specifically for women and older individuals*, and more public awareness of pickup as a form of self-care.

## Critical read
- n = 10, self-selected enthusiasts. The design can't support claims about effectiveness or dosage.
- Touch, the paper's central theme, also has negative forms in pickup (aggression, injury, gendered exclusion). The paper acknowledges barriers only in its recommendations.
