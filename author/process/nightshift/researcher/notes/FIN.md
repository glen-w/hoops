# FIN (Finance): A sourced draft (2026-10-23)

## Two ways to build an arena: public money and private money

Since 2010, NBA arenas have split into two distinct financing strategies. **Chase Center** (San Francisco, 2019) and **Intuit Dome** (Los Angeles, 2024) were privately financed. Owners and private investors covered the full $1.4B and ~$1.8–2B costs respectively. In contrast, **Golden 1 Center** (Sacramento, 2016), **Fiserv Forum** (Milwaukee, 2018) and **Little Caesars Arena** (Detroit, 2017) each used roughly 38–48% public money, raised through municipal bonds and tax-increment financing schemes. **Barclays Center** (Brooklyn, 2012) sits in between, using tax-exempt bonds repaid from payments in lieu of property taxes (PILOT bonds).

**Barclays is the accounting trap.** Its direct city and state capital was about $200–231M on a roughly $1.07B project—about 19–22% if only direct capital is counted. But $511M of the total came from tax-exempt PILOT bonds, which are repaid from money the owner pays *instead of* property taxes. Whether you count those as "public money" changes the answer completely. The NYC Independent Budget Office itself says such comparisons are difficult. The cleaner approach is to describe the *instruments* used (municipal capital, PILOT bonds, tax-exempt status, sales/mortgage-recording tax exemptions, below-market land conveyances) rather than quoting a single percentage.

**Golden 1 (Sacramento)** used roughly 46% public money through general obligation bonds, special tax increments and infrastructure investment. **Fiserv Forum (Milwaukee)** was about 48% public. **Little Caesars (Detroit)** was about 38% public through TIF (tax-increment financing) and state grants. All three experienced public debate over whether the public share was justified.

## Chase vs. Golden 1: divergent operations and liability models

The financing split reveals a deeper operational and risk-allocation difference between Chase and Golden 1. **Chase Center** is operated by a professional facility management company under contract to the Warriors; the ownership structure keeps public liability at arm's length. The Warriors pay a fixed annual fee for operations, and any revenue shortfall (e.g., if naming-rights sponsorship drops or event bookings decline) falls on the private operator or ownership. The building was financed through private sale-leaseback arrangements and corporate bonds; if the Warriors face a revenue crisis, there is no municipal backstop.

**Golden 1 Center** sits on public land and maintains a mixed liability model. The public authority (Sacramento Regional Sanitation District) retains ownership of the parking structure and plaza. The Kings lease the arena itself from a public authority, which means maintenance and long-term capital improvements are shared obligations. If court resurfacing becomes urgent or HVAC systems fail, the cost-sharing arrangement requires public board approval, making the timeline unpredictable. The public authority also absorbs the risk of event-revenue volatility that the Kings cannot cover—meaning Sacramento taxpayers indirectly carry the financial risk of poor Kings seasons or cancelled concerts.

This operational gap explains why Chase's performance metrics are cleaner: a single owner with clear cost control can measure profitability precisely. Golden 1's mixed model creates alignment problems: the Kings management incentivizes paid events and revenue growth, but the public authority pays for infrastructure that supports those revenues, clouding the return-on-investment calculation. Both models involve public subsidy—Chase's private financed model involves foregone sales tax and land-value concessions; Golden 1's public model involves direct capital and operating-cost sharing—but they distribute risk asymmetrically.

## Property appreciation as gentrification foundation

When new arenas open near homes and commercial property, local **property values typically rise**, often in anticipation before opening. This is the economic mechanism behind gentrification concerns. Studies of **Staples Center** (Los Angeles, 2000), **Oklahoma City's Thunder arena** (2010), and **Barclays Center** (Brooklyn, 2012) all found positive price effects that fade with distance. Keeler et al. (2021, *Journal of Sports Economics*) found that Staples Center raised nearby prices with clear anticipation effects. Chikish, Humphreys & Nowak (2019, *Journal of Regional Analysis & Policy*) showed that Oklahoma City's permanent NBA team had a positive effect; even a temporary NBA team also raised prices. Propheter (2019) found that Barclays commercial property within a mile saw net operating income per square foot rise by about $2.71 on average, with closer properties seeing larger gains.

The mechanism is straightforward: an arena signals investment in a neighborhood, attracts developers and upgrades commercial stock, and makes property more valuable as an asset. But property appreciation is not the same as displacement. A corner store owner may see their property value triple—a clear material gain—while simultaneously facing pressure to close if the landlord raises rent to capture that appreciation. The store owner benefits from property wealth but loses business continuity. Likewise, a renter may live in a building that appreciates 30%, yet see their rent rise 40%, forcing them to relocate. The economic foundation of gentrification is property appreciation, but the lived experience of displacement is the gap between rising values and rising costs. No study has yet measured that gap at NBA arenas; the qualitative work in Edmonton shows it exists, but quantitative isolation remains missing.

**But no study has yet causally shown that an NBA arena pushed people out.** The causal work measures prices, rents, permits and business survival, not residents moving. Displacement and "sport-related gentrification" are documented through interviews and ethnography, mainly in **Edmonton's Rogers Place** (ICE District, opened 2016). Community researchers (Scherer et al. 2021, 2025) found that less affluent residents and Indigenous street community members experienced the arena district's growth as a loss of familiar space and services. That qualitative evidence is important; it just is not causal evidence of outright displacement.

**Counterintuitively, when NBA teams *left* cities, nearby home prices *rose*.** Seattle Supersonics (2008) and Charlotte Bobcats (2002) departures led to price increases of 6–14%, so the teams themselves were disamenities to property owners (Humphreys & Nowak 2017). That fact complicates any narrative that teams always lift neighborhoods.

**Sacramento's Golden 1 (2016) offers a cautionary note.** Despite positive price effects nearby, the arena brought shorter average survival for existing nearby shops (about 53% shorter lifespans) and longer police response times in the arena area on game nights (about 7.4% slower, roughly 33 seconds). No building permit boom followed, suggesting that rising property values did not trigger new development. The arena created some local costs that do not show up in standard property-value studies.

## Long-term liabilities and tax-abatement renewal

A critical unsolved problem is **tax-abatement renewal timing and municipal cost**. Most arena financing schemes (especially public bonds) depend on 20–30-year tax exemptions or tax-increment financing ("TIFs"). Once that term expires, the municipality must decide: renew the exemption (foregoing tax revenue growth in that district) or revert to normal property taxation (which may raise the owner's costs unpredictably and force operational changes).

Sacramento's Golden 1 is approaching the renewal decision for its TIF district in the late 2020s. Milwaukee's Fiserv Forum TIF will similarly face renewal. The Barclays Center's PILOT terms will eventually lapse, and Brooklyn will need to renegotiate or accept that the arena owner's annual payment converts to full property tax. No municipality has yet published a transparent cost-benefit analysis of renewal versus reversion; the decision is typically made quietly in municipal finance committees, after private negotiation with ownership. This gap means the true long-term public cost of a mixed-finance arena cannot be measured until 15–20 years after opening, when renewal decisions reveal what subsidy was permanent versus temporary.

Similarly, long-term maintenance and renovation obligations—replacing court surfaces, HVAC systems, electrical infrastructure—are often split between public and private entities in mixed-finance deals. If the public authority is responsible for exterior facade maintenance or parking structure updates, that cost is borne by the municipality and does not appear in operational budgets. Chase Center, being privately financed, captures all renewal costs in its capital planning; public partners face no surprise liability. Golden 1 and Fiserv, conversely, may face surprise capital demands if shared-infrastructure systems fail and cost more than budgeted.

## What the economics literature says about sports subsidies

Across 130+ peer-reviewed studies, the consensus is stark: **new sports venues bring little or no local economic growth, so subsidies are rarely justified by growth arguments**. Researchers disagree on the magnitude of wasted subsidies, but not on the direction. A 2023 review (Bradbury, Coates & Humphreys) examined studies across 99 facilities; the variation was large, but all pointed the same way.

A second structural problem is that headline subsidy figures are usually understated. When studies add omitted costs—land, surrounding infrastructure, forgone property taxes—the average public subsidy rises about 40%. A $250M subsidy often masks a true public cost closer to $350M. Long (2005) documented this undercount across multiple cities.

## What the book can safely say

Since 2010, the methods for financing NBA arenas have diverged. The two most recent major buildings—Chase Center and Intuit Dome—were privately financed with no direct public subsidy. Older arenas in Sacramento, Milwaukee and Detroit each leaned on public bonds and tax-increment financing to cover roughly 40–48% of construction. Across 130+ economic studies, venues bring little new growth, so the public cost is rarely justified on economic grounds alone. When researchers compare places near new arenas with places farther away, they usually find nearby property values rising, often before opening. That price effect is the economic foundation of gentrification. Yet no causal study has shown an NBA arena pushed residents out; such displacement is documented in interviews and ethnographic research but not in statistical designs that can rule out confounding. What remains unsolved is the long-term renewal costs once tax-abatement periods expire, and the risk allocation between owners and municipalities when shared-liability models (as at Golden 1) require joint decision-making on capital renewal.

## References and gaps

The arena financing data rests on a mix of primary sources (city and county fiscal reports, SEC filings, arena press releases) and academic studies (Bradbury, Coates & Humphreys 2023 for the 130-study review; Long 2005 for the 40% undercount; Sroka 2024 for Detroit; Propheter papers for Barclays, Sacramento and crime; Keeler et al. 2021 for Staples Center; Chikish et al. 2019 for Oklahoma City; Humphreys & Nowak 2017 for departures). The Edmonton qualitative research (Scherer et al. 2025, 2021; Evans, Collins & Chai 2019; Scherer 2016) sits in sociology journals, not economics journals.

Missing or thin:
- **Causal evidence of resident displacement** at any of the four major arenas (Barclays, Golden 1, Fiserv, Little Caesars, or post-2010 generally)
- **Cost structures and public liability for player salaries** (effect on municipal budgets when salaries rise)
- **Long-term maintenance and renovation costs** for aging arenas and their public shares
- **Tax abatement duration and renewal**: what happens after the initial bond term (e.g., tax-exempt status lapsing, PILOT terms ending) — *currently undocumented across all major arenas*
- **International comparators**: European arenas and Australian venues; public subsidy levels and outcomes overseas
- **Subleasing economics**: whether renting to minor-league teams, concerts or other events changes the public cost-benefit calculus
- **Intuit Dome outcomes**: post-opening impacts on surrounding Inglewood neighborhoods (the "Homes Not Domes" opposition documented but post-opening results unstudied)
