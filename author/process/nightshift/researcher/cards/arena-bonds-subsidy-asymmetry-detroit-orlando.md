# Arena bond financing: subsidy mechanisms and competitive inequality

Little Caesars Arena (Detroit, 2018) and Amway Center (Orlando, 2017) employ distinct public financing structures that distribute fiscal risk and subsidy asymmetrically across municipalities and franchises.

**Detroit's tax-increment refunding (TI) model:** Downtown Development Authority (DDA) issued $287.4M in 2018A senior refunding bonds, leveraging property-tax increment within a defined district. This mechanism ties subsidy to real-estate appreciation within a bounded geography; default risk concentrates on the municipality if property values stagnate or gentrification reverses. The 2018 issuance refinanced prior debt, rolling forward municipal exposure into future years.

**Orlando's tourism-development tax (TDT) model:** City issued $159.9M in 2017A senior refunding bonds through a dedicated 6% tourism tax on hotel rooms (plus related transient-use streams: car rental, prepared food). This mechanism couples subsidy to visitor volume and transient spending; default risk rises acutely during tourism downturns or recession. Geographic incidence is broader but more cyclically volatile than property-tax increment anchored to a single district.

**Subsidy asymmetry and competitive inequality:** Detroit's 2018 bond par ($287.4M nominal; approximately $175M in constant 2000 dollars using ENR CCI construction index) substantially exceeds Orlando's 2017 outstanding ($159.9M; approximately $96M constant-2000). Yet Orlando's bond service depends on transient taxation—deeply unstable during recession—while Detroit's depends on property value capture in economically stressed neighborhoods. Neither structure requires franchises to bear debt service; both externalize fiscal risk entirely to public treasuries. This asymmetry creates structural competitive inequality rooted not in on-court performance but in municipal fiscal capacity and tax base composition.
