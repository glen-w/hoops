# Local Broadcast Rights and Revenue Sharing in the NBA (1960–2000)

The NBA's broadcast revenue model institutionalised a two-tiered system: the league negotiated national contracts yielding shared revenue, whilst individual franchises retained unilateral control and full revenue from local broadcasts. By the late 1980s, approximately 80 per cent of money distributed to teams originated from national television contracts {Bell, n.d.}, with teams receiving roughly $6.8 million per season from equal distribution. However, teams retained 100 per cent of revenues from local broadcast contracts covering games not shown nationally {Bell, n.d.}. This asymmetry created strategic incentives: teams sought to maximise local exposure, whilst the league restricted such coverage to prevent undermining national contracts' exclusivity. The NBA's 1980 Board of Governors resolution limiting each team to broadcasting 41 games on free, over-the-air stations exemplified this constraint {Bell, n.d.}. The Chicago Bulls' 1980s shift to WGN-TV, whose superstation signal reached national cable audiences, prompted urgent NBA intervention, illustrating tension between local market expansion and league-wide revenue protection {Bell, n.d.}. The 1988–1990 CBS/TBS contract ($71 million per season) and 1990–1991 NBC/TNT deal ($180 million annually) reflected escalating national rights values, further emphasising the league's centralisation strategy.
