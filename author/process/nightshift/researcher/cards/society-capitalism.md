**Society: Capitalism and labour in professional basketball**

Professional basketball operates within capitalist frameworks that fundamentally define athlete compensation, franchise ownership structures, and league-wide revenue distribution. The NBA's Collective Bargaining Agreement explicitly determines players' collective share of basketball-related income (BRI) against the interests of franchise owners and league governance. Franchise valuations have risen dramatically from approximately $300 million in 2000 to over $2 billion by 2025, yet player compensation has remained contested through multiple lockouts and strikes.

Scholarly analysis should address: athletes as capital assets (contracts, trades, insurance valuations), labour relations embedded in ownership structures (minimum wages, luxury tax mechanisms, draft systems), and how capitalism shapes franchise decision-making on community investment and worker equity. Wage disparity across arena workers—food service, facilities management—reveals capitalist hierarchies within the sport itself. A sustained capitalism framework connects player activism, franchise profitability, and labour visibility (or erasure) in sports media narratives.
