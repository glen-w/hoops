The player agent industry emerged as a central force in NBA labour relations between 1960 and 2000. C. C. Pyle, regarded as the first 'sports agent,' negotiated early athlete contracts, but the modern agent profession crystallised when Mark McCormack established the International Management Group (IMG) in 1960, which rapidly became dominant in representing sports celebrities {Smart, 2007} [52ENVUDK]. By 1990, *Sports Illustrated* identified McCormack as "the most powerful man in sport," reflecting agents' growing influence over athlete commercial affairs and collective bargaining strategy {Smart, 2007} [52ENVUDK].

This infrastructural shift proved decisive in the NBA's labour politics. Under the reserve system, clubs held exclusive rights to players' services, creating monopsonistic exploitation identified by {Rottenberg, 1956} and quantified by {Scully, 1969} {Hill, 2001} [X7L76G7E]. The NBPA's victory in securing free agency, following the Supreme Court's Oscar Robertson ruling, fundamentally redistributed power between owners and players, with agents becoming increasingly central to negotiating that distribution. By 1995, prominent agents like David Falk represented both Michael Jordan and Patrick Ewing in their attempted NBPA decertification during the ownership lockout, demonstrating how individual agents had become pivotal actors in the league's labour disputes [ZJBI4A46; 3E5DEJAP].
