## 1. Two Models: Private vs. Subsidized (post-2010)

### Privately Financed (Bay Area and LA)
- **Chase Center (Warriors, 2019):** $1.4B cost, $0 public, team self-financed
- **Intuit Dome (Clippers, 2024):** $1.8–2B cost, $0 direct capital; city land sale terms not found. Team paid $100M community benefits; no traditional subsidy
- **Pattern:** Tech-rich markets, wealthy ownership, private capital

### Heavily Subsidized (Midwest and Southwest)
- **Golden 1 Center (Kings, Sacramento, 2016):** $558.2M cost, $255M public (45.7%), city lease-revenue bonds (Sep 2015), repaid from parking then lease + tax increment
- **Fiserv Forum (Bucks, Milwaukee, 2018):** $524M cost, $250M public (47.7%), state $80M + county + city TIF + Wisconsin Center District bonds
- **Little Caesars Arena (Pistons+Red Wings, Detroit, 2017):** $862.9M cost, $324M public (37.5%), Detroit DDA tax-increment bonds capturing school taxes
- **Pattern:** Tax-increment financing, city/state bonds, school tax capture (controversial in Detroit)

### Ambiguous: PILOT Bonds and Tax Exemptions (Brooklyn)
- **Barclays Center (Nets, 2012):** ~$1.07B cost, direct capital ~$200–231M (NYC+state). But $511M came via tax-exempt **PILOT bonds** (payments-in-lieu of taxes), repaid from substitute property-tax payments. Property-tax exemption and MTA land value also involved. **Public % ambiguous (19–22% direct vs. much higher if PILOTs and exemptions count)**
- NYC Independent Budget Office itself says these subsidies are hard to compare

## 2. Consensus: Economic Impact Is Minimal

**Literature review (130+ studies):** Bradbury, Coates & Humphreys (2023) *J Econ Surveys* 37(4):1389–1431 [XDMTJR8C]
- New venues bring **very limited local economic growth**
- Subsidies rarely justified by growth claims
- Consensus is robust across US, Canada, Europe

**Hidden costs raise average subsidy ~40%:** Long (2005) "Full Count" *J Sports Econ* 6(2):119–143 [CB2ZQ4CG]
- Headline public costs ($126M average across 99 facilities) understate by ~40% when omitted items are counted: land, infrastructure, forgone property/sales/mortgage-recording taxes
- Updated figure: ~$177M average
- **Implication:** official post-2010 subsidy figures (e.g., "Golden 1 is 45.7% public") miss land, parking revenue, TIF future forgone, exemptions

## 3. Case Study: Detroit's Little Caesars (DDA Tax-Increment Capture)

**Source:** Sroka (2024) *Social Science Quarterly* [ASQSSF2Q], DOI 10.1111/ssqu.13424

- Built 2017; Pistons moved in as tenants
- DDA tax-increment bonds captured **school district taxes** during and after city bankruptcy
- Illustrates how TIF can siphon education funding into arena subsidies
- Controversial: trade-off between arena and schools not transparent

## 6. What the Book Can Say (Economic Impact)

Since 2010, NBA arenas have been financed in starkly different ways. Two Bay Area and LA buildings were privately financed: Chase Center (~$1.4B) and Intuit Dome (~$1.8–2B). Sacramento, Milwaukee, and Detroit each put roughly 38–48% public money into their new arenas, mostly through city bonds and tax-increment schemes. Brooklyn's Barclays Center used tax-exempt bonds repaid from property-tax substitutes, which makes its "public share" a matter of definition. Across 130+ studies, economists find that such venues produce little new local growth, and official subsidy totals usually leave out land, infrastructure, and forgone taxes—likely adding ~40% to the headline figure.

## 7. Arena Gentrification and Property-Value Effects

**Bottom line (for manuscript):** Studies comparing places near new arenas with places farther away usually find nearby property values going up, often before the doors open. That is the economic engine of gentrification. But no study has yet shown, with a causal design, that an NBA arena pushed residents out. Sacramento's new arena brought shorter lives for existing nearby shops, slower police response on game nights and no building boom. In Edmonton, researchers who interviewed residents describe a "sport-related gentrification" whose costs fell unevenly, especially on the area's Indigenous urban street community and less affluent residents. Price rises are well established; displacement is plausible and documented in qualitative work, but it has not been measured.
