# STADIUM: NBA Arena Construction, Financing, and Revenue

## Public Financing Trends

### Historical Pattern: Arenas vs. Outdoor Stadiums
Propheter (n.d.) documents divergent public-financing rates [S1]:
- **1970s–1980s arenas:** 35% publicly financed (after removing expansion teams)
- **1970s–1980s baseball stadiums:** 100% publicly financed
- **1970s–1980s football stadiums:** 87% publicly financed
- **1990s trend:** Arenas continued more private financing, outdoor stadiums remained public-dependent
- **Early 2000s reversal:** Basketball arenas "again mainly financed with subsidies"

### Aggregate Public Funding (1990–1999)
Groothuis et al. (n.d.) [S4]:
- 46 stadiums built or substantially renovated for four major leagues (1990–1998)
- Additional 49 under construction/planning (by end 1999)
- **$21.7 billion total spent on 95 buildings**
- **Approximately two-thirds ($14.5B+) came from government sources**

Humphreys & Nowak (n.d.) [S2]:
- 64 new sports facilities built for NBA, MLB, NFL, NHL (1991–2006)
- "Most involved public subsidies; many were 100% publicly financed"

## Financing Mechanisms

### Tax Increment Financing (TIF)
- Public bonds issued to cover land, infrastructure, construction
- Repayment from incremental property tax revenues over long period
- **Examples:** Rogers Place (Edmonton), KFC Yum! Center (Louisville), proposed Chicago downtown arena, Milwaukee arena [S2]

### Broad-Based and Special Taxes
Swindell & Rosentraub (1998) [S3]:
- Sales taxes, property taxes, taxes on alcohol/tobacco, hotel rooms, car rentals
- "Commonplace" for building or operating facilities
- Teams typically retain most or all facility revenues

### User-Fee Models
Swindell & Rosentraub propose special user tax district [S3]:
- "Those who benefit from the facilities bear the cost"
- Revenue sources: concession fees, parking taxes, other user charges
- Could finance without broad-based taxes
