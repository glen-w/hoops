# STADIUM — arena financing and revenue (grounded from paperful_ask)

- Public financing of professional sports venues begins in the 1950s–60s, as aging early-1900s facilities could not be rehabilitated for league expansion [S1, CGMDP7GN].
- Through the 1950s most teams played in privately owned venues; public involvement was minimal [S3, 9795GQW9].
- Local officials were more inclined to award subsidies for football and baseball stadiums; excluding the four 1980s NBA expansion teams, only 35% of 1970s–80s arenas were publicly financed versus 100% of baseball stadiums and 87% of football stadiums [S1, CGMDP7GN].
- From 1991 to 2006, 64 new NBA/MLB/NFL/NHL facilities were built, most with some public subsidy and many 100% publicly financed [S2, 3EGEXSSS].
- Tax Increment Financing is highlighted as an increasingly popular mechanism [S2, 3EGEXSSS].
- In the first decade of the 2000s, basketball arenas were again mainly subsidy-financed, while pressure for baseball and football subsidies never let up [S1, CGMDP7GN].
- It is commonplace for cities, counties, and states to use broad-based or special taxes to build or operate facilities, with teams retaining most or all revenues; some communities invested over US$500 million [S3, 9795GQW9].
- Contingent-valuation surveys find public goods from a minor-league baseball stadium and a new arena fail to justify significant public subsidies [S4, ZQQVN8DQ].
