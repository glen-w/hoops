# Franchise Ownership and Capital Structures, 1950–2000

Early NBA franchises operated as personal enterprises, typically owned by individual wealthy entrepreneurs with direct financial liability. Owner capital absorption of operating losses was the norm; risk was unshared. By the 1970s, tax policy shifted incentives: depreciation allowances on player contracts and facilities made franchises attractive vehicles for passive investors seeking loss deductions. Syndicated ownership structures (limited partnerships) expanded in the 1980s, enabling distributed capital and risk diffusion. The 1984 salary cap directly constrained labour costs, forcing owner returns toward revenue growth rather than payroll arbitrage. Luxury taxes introduced post-1998 penalised high payroll spending, reshaping capital allocation discipline. Regional broadcast rights emerged as primary value drivers, particularly for franchises in large media markets. By 2000, franchise valuations reflected anticipated broadcast revenue over historical operating performance, signalling a fundamental shift in investor logic. Institutional capital and investment groups began acquiring stakes, marking the transition from personal ventures to structured capital assets, mirroring broader American commercialisation of sport.
