# NBA Franchise Economics and Wealth Inequality, 1950–2026

Franchise ownership concentrates wealth among entrepreneurs with existing capital. Flint's 1987 analysis of 22 NBA owners in the *Forbes* 400 found the largest sources of owner wealth were oil/energy (27.3%), real estate development (27.3%), and communications (13.6%)—sectors requiring substantial existing capital to enter [S1]. This pattern reflects what Blaine (1984) observed: 44% of professional sports franchise owners across five leagues had amassed wealth through real estate [S1].

Tax incentives historically attracted investors seeking to shelter capital gains. Nixon (1974) detailed the depreciation mechanism: a $50,000 franchise fee against a $6 million player roster generated $600,000 annual tax write-offs; at 50% corporate tax rates, owners saved $300,000 yearly for ten years [S8]. This structure, combined with legal exemptions from antitrust enforcement, attracted what Nixon termed "shrewd businessmen with surplus capital to invest, investments to shelter, and personal income tax liabilities to reduce" [S8].

Arena financing since the 1990s furthered wealth concentration by shifting public capital risk to municipal bonds while directing franchise profits to private owners—raising sustainability questions about public returns on sports investment and labor equity in professional basketball.

**Sources:** S1 Flint (1987); S8 Nixon (1974); S2–S7 cross-subsidy and economic impact studies.
