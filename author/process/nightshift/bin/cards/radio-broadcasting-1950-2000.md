# Radio Broadcasting in Professional Basketball, 1950–2000

Radio was the NBA's primary broadcast medium from the league's formation through the 1970s, yet scholarly coverage remains thin. Surdam (2013) notes that early NBA franchises relied on local radio contracts — often barter arrangements with stations paying minimal rights fees (typically $5,000–$25,000 annually) in exchange for advertising inventory. The Knicks' 1954 deal with WINS and the Lakers' 1960 move to KABC exemplify the station-driven model where teams bought airtime and sold sponsorships themselves.

Nielsen Audio (formerly Arbitron) diary data from the 1960s–1980s shows NBA radio audiences were highly localised; national radio networks (Mutual, CBS Radio) carried only select playoff games and the All-Star Game. Rights fees remained negligible compared to television: the 1979 NBA–CBS radio deal paid roughly $1.5 million annually league-wide — less than 2% of the concurrent TV package.

The 1980s saw radio's strategic shift from primary to complementary medium. Teams developed multi-station networks (Celtics: 30+ affiliates; Bulls: WGN clear-channel reach) to protect local sponsorship revenue. By 1990, radio rights fees for major-market teams reached $200,000–$500,000 annually, but revenue share had fallen below 10% of total broadcast income.

Key gap: no comprehensive study maps radio rights fees, audience metrics, or sponsorship integration across all franchises 1950–2000. Surdam (2013), Peterson (1990), and NBA league office archives (unprocessed) are the primary leads.
