NBA franchise ownership differs structurally from operating management. Per Fort (2024), owners derive their primary income not from day-to-day operating earnings but from capital appreciation of franchise values. Quirk and Fort (1992) document average annual NBA franchise-price escalation of roughly 16% (1950–1990), peaking above 30% in the 1980s—rates far exceeding inflation and general asset appreciation.

Flint (1987) examined ownership composition using 1983–84 corporate directories, finding that owners' wealth concentrated in real estate and development (27.3%), oil and energy (27.3%), and communications (13.6%). Ownership predates the franchise purchase; franchises are wealth-storage vehicles within entrepreneurial portfolios.

This ownership asymmetry is amplified by public arena financing. When municipalities deploy tax-increment financing (TIF) or tourism development taxes (TDT) to construct arenas, taxpayers absorb construction risk, cost overruns, and revenue volatility. Owners, meanwhile, capture the franchises' capital appreciation—which benefit from public infrastructure investment—while their operating margins remain negotiable. The public finances risk; private owners realize monopoly-protected gains.
