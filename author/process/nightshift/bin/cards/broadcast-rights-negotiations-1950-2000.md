# Broadcast Rights Negotiations 1950–2000

{Smith, 2018; PENDING}

The NBA's broadcast rights trajectory from 1950 to 2000 moved from local radio deals to national television auctions that reshaped franchise economics. Early 1950s contracts were fragmented — individual teams negotiated radio and nascent TV rights with local stations, yielding minimal revenue. The 1954 NBC deal (US$1.5m over two years) marked the first national package, but coverage remained selective. 

The 1970s brought competitive bidding: CBS secured rights in 1973 (US$27m/three years), then TBS entered in 1982 with cable's first national package (US$20m/two years). The 1988 CBS renewal at US$173m/four years signalled the modern rights explosion, driven by Bird–Magic rivalry, Jordan's rise, and cable penetration. NBC's 1990 deal (US$600m/four years) and 1994 renewal (US$750m/four years) cemented national TV as the primary revenue engine. 

Regional sports networks (RSNs) emerged in the 1980s — Prism, SportsChannel, later Fox Sports Net — creating dual revenue streams. Teams retained local rights while sharing national pools. The 1995 expansion (Toronto, Vancouver) and 1998 lockout coincided with rights fees peaking; the 1998 NBC/TBS deal (US$2.64bn/four years) reflected peak optimism before fragmentation. 

Key drivers: scarcity of live sports inventory, advertiser demand for male 18–49 demographics, cable subscription leverage, and league-controlled packaging. Franchise revenue share from national TV grew from <5% (1970) to ~30% (2000). Local RSN deals varied wildly — Lakers, Knicks, Bulls commanded premiums; small markets lagged. 

Primary sources: NBA league office archives (negotiation minutes), network press releases, *Sports Business Journal* (1994–), *Television/Radio Age*, FCC filings, team financial statements (litigation discovery), *Broadcasting & Cable* yearbooks.
