**NBA Franchise Capital and Valuation, 1960–2000**

Franchise valuations in professional basketball rose substantially through systematic league expansion and revenue consolidation. Expansion fees climbed from $2 million (Chicago 1966) to $32.5 million (Vancouver/Toronto 1995), reflecting market-based franchise placement criteria: metropolitan population, television audience size, and local owner capitalisation. The NBA Board of Governors selected expansion markets through geographic growth analysis and broadcast market penetration studies. Expansion placement decisions targeted Sun Belt cities and television markets: New Orleans (1974), Dallas (1980), and Miami/Charlotte (1988) additions tracked demographic shifts and television expansion.

Franchise asset values derived from four revenue streams: gate receipts (tickets, concessions), local broadcasting (team-controlled regional agreements), national television (league-negotiated pooled contracts), and ancillary income (sponsorship, facility naming). Geographic market size produced substantial revenue disparities: large-market franchises (Los Angeles, New York, Boston) generated 2–3× revenues of small-market teams through premium pricing, regional media monopolies, and corporate arena partnerships. The 1976 ABA absorption eliminated competitive league threats, securing the NBA's monopsony control over player labour and monopoly control over broadcasting rights. Ownership remained concentrated in real estate and manufacturing capital through the 1980s; corporate sports equity funds emerged only when national television valuations exceeded $2 billion annually. By 2000, franchise valuations tracked television audience metrics and national contract growth more closely than local market fundamentals or on-court performance.
