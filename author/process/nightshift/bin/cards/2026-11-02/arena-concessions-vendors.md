# Arena Concessions and Vendor Revenue Partnerships

Most NBA franchises outsource food and beverage operations to large hospitality contractors—Aramark, Compass Group, Delaware North—under revenue-sharing agreements rather than directly operating concessions. Vendors typically retain 40–60% of gross concession revenue, while franchises capture the remainder, creating profit incentives aligned with volume and pricing rather than customer satisfaction. Vendor contracts specify performance targets (per-capita spending) and operational standards (service speed, product range) but grant contractors wide discretion over staffing, supplier selection, and wage levels.

The economics of vendor-managed concessions vary sharply by venue age, market tier, and seating configuration. Premium arenas (post-2010 builds, large markets) with luxury suites and club seating generate outsized food revenue through corporate catering and exclusive vendors; older venues with single-level seating generate proportionally less. Golden State, Dallas, and some LA-market franchises have experimented with hybrid models: contracting premium catering to celebrity-chef partners while delegating main-concourse commodity operations to traditional vendors—positioning food service as brand differentiation rather than fungible revenue.

Scholarly attention to vendor economics remains limited; worker-condition critiques (seasonal employment, low wages, minimal benefits) increasingly frame arena concessions as part of broader public-subsidy inequality debates, yet franchise profitability and vendor margins receive minimal public scrutiny.
