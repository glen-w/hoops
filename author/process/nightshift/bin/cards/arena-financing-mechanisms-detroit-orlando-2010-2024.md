Public arena financing structures reshaped franchise competitive capacity post-2000. The Little Caesars Arena (Detroit, 2017) financed through senior tax-increment refunding bonds ($287.4M par, 2018A), tying debt service to TIF-captured downtown revenue. The Amway Center (Orlando, 2010) employed tourism development tax (TDT) revenue and senior refunding bonds ($159.9M outstanding, 2024), creating distinct public-private splits.

These structures affected franchise flexibility differently. Detroit's TIF model concentrated debt service on downtown increments, constraining operating budgets when increments stalled. Orlando's TDT model linked arena debt to hotel occupancy cycles, isolating arena funding from general city budgets but creating volatility.

Cost inflation compounded debt cycles. ENR CCI (construction cost index, 20-city) rose from 6,221 (2000) to 10,847 (2026)—a 74% increase. Both franchises refinanced bonds multiple times, extending maturity and increasing total interest expense—mechanics invisible to attendance but material to payroll flexibility.

Public financing frameworks thus embed arena affordability into franchise competitive capacity, orthogonal to salary cap mechanics. These case studies illustrate how municipal bond structures and construction cost inflation constrain competitive parity independent of league-level salary cap rules.
