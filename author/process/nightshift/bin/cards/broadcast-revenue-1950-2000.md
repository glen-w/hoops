# Broadcast Revenue and NBA Financial Structure, 1950–2000

Television broadcasting became the NBA's dominant revenue stream, reshaping franchise economics from 1950 onwards. By 1990–91, combined NBC and TNT contracts exceeded $180 million, representing approximately 80 per cent of all league-distributed revenue (Bell). By 1998–99, these networks had committed $2.4 billion through 2001–02 (Silver).

The NBA employed a hybrid revenue model: the league negotiated national broadcasting rights centrally whilst franchises retained 100 per cent of revenues from local broadcasts not nationally covered (Bell). To maximise national contract value, in 1980 the Board of Governors capped each franchise at 41 over-the-air, free-broadcast games per season (Bell). This market limitation reflected the league's antitrust-protected cartel power, formalised by the Sports Broadcasting Act 1961 (Public Law 87–331), which exempted sports leagues from antitrust scrutiny in joint broadcast negotiations (Weiner).

Television-driven revenue growth directly correlated with franchise valuations. Between 1950 and 1990, NBA franchise values increased at 16 per cent annually, accelerating to over 30 per cent yearly during the 1980s—a trajectory directly attributable to rising national television income (Fort, 2024).
