# NBA Broadcast Rights and Revenue Architecture (1960–2000)

The foundation of modern NBA broadcast revenue lies in pooled rights sanctioned by the 1961 Sports Broadcasting Act. A 1953 court ruling had initially prohibited individual clubs from jointly negotiating television contracts, eliminating competitive bidding; Congress reversed this through statutory exemption to allow leagues to act as cartels in selling broadcast rights [Weiner, 2002; ABGHY7JN]. This legislative cartel framework enabled national network contracts to be negotiated collectively rather than individually by franchise.

By 1980, the NBA Board of Governors strategically limited each team to 41 free-broadcast games annually to maximize national-contract value [Bell, n.d.; N4V2QEU3]. The 1988–1990 CBS/TBS deal yielded $142.5 million total (c. $71 million per season); the 1990–91 NBC/TNT contract doubled that to $180 million, with NBC carrying 22 regular-season games, TNT 50, and both splitting approximately 30 playoff games each [N4V2QEU3]. National broadcast revenue was distributed equally among the 27-team league, establishing an equalisation mechanism that underpinned competitive balance and franchise stability through the 1990s.

This architecture—pooled negotiation, regulated terrestrial limits, cable expansion, and equal sharing—generated league-wide revenue growth whilst constraining individual franchise leverage, a structural innovation distinct from baseball and football [VCI67TB8].
