# Broadcast Rights Revenue and NBA Franchise Economics, 1960–2000

Television broadcast revenue fundamentally reshaped NBA franchise economics. By 1990–91, national contracts with NBC and TNT exceeded $180 million combined, with revenue distributed equally across 27 teams at approximately $6.8 million per franchise—constituting 80 per cent of all league-wide communal distribution {N4V2QEU3}. From 1998–99 through 2001–02, NBC and Turner Networks paid $2.4 billion for national broadcasting rights {PJ35JCEH, CLVYPM2N}.

Revenue-sharing mechanisms sharply distinguished national and local streams. Teams retained 100 per cent of local broadcast revenues—a critical asymmetry favouring large media markets {N4V2QEU3}. Turner's mid-1990s contract ($350 million fixed fee plus 50 per cent of advertising revenue) exemplified dual-revenue models adopted across national deals.

The 1996–97 WGN superstation settlement split advertising revenues (50–50) without requiring annual rights fees, creating a hybrid structure for national cable distribution {N4V2QEU3}.

These broadcast licensing mechanisms created persistent financial inequality across franchises, with the transition from local to national television rights becoming central to franchise valuations and relocation economics by 2000.
