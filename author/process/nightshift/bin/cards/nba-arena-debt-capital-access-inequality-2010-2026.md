# NBA Arena Debt Capital Access Inequality, 2010–2026

(Grokbot consolidation 2026-11-01: Little Caesars 2018, Amway Center 2017)

Detroit and Orlando arena bonds illustrate how public revenue sources—ticket income, tourism development taxes, convention center revenues—create asymmetric debt capacity across franchises. The Little Caesars Arena (2018, Pistons/Red Wings) used $287M in senior tax-increment refunding bonds backed by new-arena ticket surcharges and existing tax increments; concurrently, the Amway Center (2017, Magic) refinanced $159M through Tourism Development Tax revenues, linking debt service directly to hotel/visitor spending.

This structure reveals a hidden disparity: franchises in high-tourism, high-tax-capacity markets (Orlando's visitor economy, Detroit's revitalization zone) can lever public revenue streams to raise cheaper debt than franchises in lower-tax markets. Senior bond ratings and coupon structures reflect this asymmetry—tax-backed debt typically offers better terms than arena-revenue-only bonds. Over time, franchises with preferential access to public tax revenue can fund facility improvements, player salaries, and competitive upgrades more cheaply than peers unable to command similar public subsidy.

The mechanism is obscured because framing emphasizes "public-private partnerships" rather than capital inequality. Yet bond prospectuses show that franchise competitive position increasingly reflects local tax authority and fiscal capacity, not just management acumen or market size.
