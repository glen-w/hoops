# STADIUM: A sourced draft (2026-10-23)

## Home court and the fans

Home-court advantage in the NBA is real. Teams win about 58–60% of their games at home. When COVID shut down arenas and fans stopped attending in 2020, that advantage collapsed: home teams won only about 50–51% with no crowd. The difference was about 2.1 points per game with fans vs 0.44 points without. The most reliable estimate is that every 1,000 fans add roughly 1.7 points to home-team advantage (Ganz & Allsop 2023, *Journal of Sports Economics*).

But **the fans work mainly through the players, not the referees**. When researchers used the NBA's own Last Two Minute Reports and studied call patterns directly, they found little evidence that officials had been systematically biased toward home teams when crowds were roaring. Gong (2022) compared close games with and without fans and found no crowd-caused bias in calls. Szabó (2022) looked at the NBA, NFL and NHL together and found that home wins dropped during lockdowns, but the calls did not become measurably fairer to the visiting team. Leota et al. (2022) found that rebounding effort—a player behavior—mediated the fan effect, not referee bias. Ehrlich & Potter (2023) studied attendance variation across 2020-21 and found that empty arenas erased home advantage while small crowds kept most of it; officials were not measurably influenced.

Before COVID, the best basketball evidence of referee favouritism concerned discretionary calls. Price, Remer & Stone (2012) found that home teams, and teams trailing in a game or series, received more of the judgment-call turnovers. But the magnitude was small compared to the overall home-advantage effect.

**Soccer tells a starkly different story.** There, empty stadiums clearly made referees measurably fairer to away teams. Pettersson-Lidbom & Priks (2010) studied Italian soccer after violence bans cleared stadiums; Scoppa (2021) covered five European leagues; and Bryson et al. (2021) analyzed 6,481 matches across 17 countries. All found that away teams benefited when referees called games without crowds. The mechanism is different: in basketball, the physical demands (rebounding, sprinting, screen-setting) depend heavily on intensity and adrenaline. In soccer, the judgment calls (fouls, handballs, offsides) are more vulnerable to crowd noise and referee pressure.

**European basketball is mixed.** EuroLeague home advantage fell from about 70% to about 51% during COVID, with a change in free-throw-attempt advantage (Paulauskas et al. 2022). In Spain's ACB, fouls shifted but EuroLeague showed no foul effect (Jiménez et al. 2025).

## The arena as a neighborhood anchor

New NBA arenas have become catalysts for neighborhood attention and sometimes unequal change. When Golden 1 opened in Sacramento in 2016, nearby home prices rose (a positive signal for property wealth). But existing retail shops within a half-mile survived about 53% shorter on average. Police response times in the arena area on game nights slowed by about 7.4% (roughly 33 seconds longer). There was no building permit boom despite the rising property values—suggesting that rising values did not translate into new construction.

In **Brooklyn**, the Barclays Center (2012) brought measurable commercial benefits to nearby property within a mile: net operating income per square foot rose by about $2.71 on average, and closer properties saw larger gains. Yet no causal study measures whether tenants moved out or residents were priced out around any NBA arena.

In **Edmonton**, community researchers who interviewed residents of the downtown ICE District (Rogers Place arena, 2016) documented what they call "sport-related gentrification"—how rising property values, private security, police presence and fan crowds affected less affluent residents and the Indigenous street community, pushing some to seek services elsewhere (Scherer et al. 2021, 2025; Evans, Collins & Chai 2019). That qualitative evidence is important; it just is not causal evidence of outright displacement.

**When NBA teams *left* cities, nearby home prices *rose*.** Seattle (Supersonics, 2008) and Charlotte (Bobcats, 2002) saw price increases of 6–14% after the teams departed—meaning the teams themselves were disamenities to property owners. That complicates the narrative that arenas always lift neighborhoods.

The research consensus is that **arena-driven property appreciation is not proof of displacement**. Rising values are the mechanism behind gentrification, but displacement itself—people losing housing or businesses closing because of rising costs—remains difficult to measure. It happens, as qualitative research shows, but causal statistics have not yet isolated it.

## What remains to be built

The STADIUM chapter is essentially a sketch. It needs:

1. **The experience of attending**: climate control, seating, sightlines, concessions, bathrooms, accessibility—how arenas work *inside*
2. **Naming rights and corporate branding**: how commercial naming shapes community attachment (e.g., Crypto.com Arena vs the Forum; Golden 1 vs Sleep Train Arena)
3. **Food, drink and concessions**: price escalation, regional cuisines, the economics of arena food service
4. **Historic arenas**: the functional lives and closures of older venues (Seattle Center Coliseum, San Antonio's old Memorial Coliseum, etc.)
5. **International venues**: how arenas in Europe and Asia serve the game differently
6. **Safety and security**: post-9/11 arena design, health protocols, fan violence
7. **Logistics**: how 41 home games are scheduled, setup/teardown, playing surface maintenance
8. **The London question**: what it means for an NBA team to call an arena 20,000 miles away "home"

## References and limitations

The evidence above comes from natural experiments (Ganz & Allsop 2023, Gong 2022, Szabó 2022, Leota et al. 2022 on home advantage; Ehrlich & Potter 2023 on small crowds), hedonic property studies (Propheter 2019, 2020; Keeler et al. 2021; Chikish et al. 2019), cross-country sports comparisons (Pettersson-Lidbom & Priks 2010; Scoppa 2021; Bryson et al. 2021) and qualitative community research (Scherer et al. 2021, 2025; Evans et al. 2019).

Missing:
- **Causal evidence of resident displacement** (moves, eviction, demographic change) near any NBA arena
- **Concessions and food economics**: pricing, profit margins, cultural representation in arena menus
- **Disability access and venue design**: empirical comparison of arena accessibility standards and outcomes
- **Heat, noise and air quality** around arenas on game nights and their health effects
- **Climate and arena design**: how architectural decisions affect player comfort and injury risk (humidity, court surface, ventilation)
- **Long-term economic effects** on surrounding retail and housing beyond five years post-opening
- **International arena operations**: how foreign NBA venues (London O2, Paris events) differ in management, revenue and community impact
- **Technical fouls and ejections in home-advantage context**: no study has measured whether fans or refs influence the rate of technical calls that lead to ejections
