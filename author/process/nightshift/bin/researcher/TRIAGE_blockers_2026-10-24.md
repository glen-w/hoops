### 3. **Technical fouls and fan influence on call rates** (STADIUM)
**Gap:** No study measures whether fans (through noise/atmosphere) cause officials to call more technical fouls on visiting teams.

**Why it matters:** Home-court advantage is real (~2.1 points per game in normal conditions). The STADIUM chapter found that fans work mainly through player intensity (rebounding, effort) not referee bias in field-goal or foul calls. But technical fouls—discretionary calls for unsportsmanlike conduct, profanity, ejections—were not studied in the COVID natural experiment.

**Blocker detail:**
- Price et al. (2012) found small home-team bias in discretionary "judgment calls" (turnovers), but not technicals specifically
- Gong (2022) found no crowd effect on overall call bias; Szabó (2022) confirmed this across NBA/NHL/NFL
- No study examined technical foul call rates pre-COVID vs. during COVID (empty arenas)
- Technical fouls are rare and harder to study; they may have different mechanisms than field fouls

**Escalation path:**
- Target: NBA public data + *Journal of Sports Economics*, *Sports Management Review*, sports analytics conferences
- Data source: Last Two Minute Report database (available), technical foul frequency by team/arena
- **Action:** Conduct quick analysis (compare 2019 technical foul rates by arena vs. 2020 empty-arena rates) OR ask for SBJ/ESPN research reports on this specific question

**Interim manuscript language:**
> "Technical fouls—discretionary calls for unsportsmanlike conduct—remain unstudied in the context of crowd effects. Whether fans influence call rates for technical fouls is unknown."

**Mark as:** Can be partially answered via internal data analysis (needs Rec/Flint harness) or escalated for third-party research (2–3 weeks)

### 4. **Causal displacement from arenas** (FIN)
**Gap:** No peer-reviewed study causally shows that an NBA arena pushed residents or businesses out of the surrounding neighborhood.

**Why it matters:** Property values near arenas rise (Keeler et al. 2021, Propheter 2019, Chikish et al. 2019). Rising property values are the mechanism of gentrification. But causality is different from mechanism. Qualitative research (Scherer et al. 2025, Edmonton) documents displacement stories; causal studies isolate the arena effect on displacement rates.

**Blocker detail:**
- Property value studies show rising prices near arenas (well-established)
- Displacement literature exists for other urban anchors (transit, universities, stadiums in other sports) but not for NBA arenas
- Qualitative research (Edmonton's ICE District, Scherer et al.) documents displacement interviews, but that is not causal evidence
- Causal design challenge: counterfactual (what would have happened without the arena) is hard to construct; propensity-score matching or diff-in-diff designs require detailed microdata on residents/businesses

**Escalation path:**
- Target: *Journal of Urban Economics*, *Urban Studies*, *Journal of Regional Science*, real-estate/housing journals
- Publishers: JSTOR, Elsevier, academic society journals
- Keywords: "arena displacement", "sports gentrification", "sports-induced displacement", "event venue neighborhood effects"
- Cross-reference: non-NBA stadium literature (college football stadiums, Minor League Baseball) may have causal designs applicable to NBA
- **Action:** Urban economics research networks, universities with real-estate or displacement expertise (University of Pennsylvania, MIT, UC Berkeley)

**Interim manuscript language:**
> "Rising property values near arenas are well-documented. But whether arenas causally displace residents or businesses remains unmeasured. Qualitative research (Edmonton, 2016–2025) documents displacement experiences; causal statistical designs have not isolated the arena effect."

**Mark as:** Awaiting external discovery (3–4 weeks); if not obtained, mark as "causal evidence unavailable"
