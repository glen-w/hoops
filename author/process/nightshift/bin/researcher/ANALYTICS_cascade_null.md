# Testing the Cascade Hypothesis: Fatigue → Kinematics → Reinvestment → Performance

The "cascade hypothesis" proposes a causal chain: fatigue alters free-throw kinematics, which triggers conscious-control strategies (reinvestment), which in turn degrades performance. This hypothesis sits at the intersection of three literatures: fatigue (exercise physiology), kinematics (biomechanics), and reinvestment (sport psychology). **A complete test requires all three links to be measured in the same cohort. To date, this has not been done in basketball.**

## What Each Literature Contributes

| Link | Evidence | Gap |
|---|---|---|
| **Fatigue → kinematics** | Small-sample studies (n ≈ 10) show wrist angular velocity may change under fatigue (Rusdiana et al. 2020) but joint angles do not (Uygur et al. 2010). Both null and positive findings exist. | No large-sample study; no variability measure in Rusdiana; direction disputed. |
| **Kinematics → reinvestment** | Malhotra et al. (2015) in golf putting found higher MSRS scores go with *lower* kinematic variability (opposite sign). No basketball study. | Golf finding contradicts the hypothesis. No basketball pairing of kinematics and reinvestment scales. |
| **Reinvestment → performance decrement** | Kinrade et al. (2010) validated DSRS on team-sport players and coaches' choking ratings (r = .74). Jackson et al. (2006) found high reinvesters more prone to failure under pressure in field-hockey and soccer. No basketball-specific test. | No basketball free-throw study of reinvestment and performance. Reinvestment scales were developed on other sports. |

## Outcome-Level Test: Workload Does Not Amplify Pressure Choking

A necessary (if not sufficient) test of the cascade hypothesis is whether workload amplifies pressure-induced performance drops. If fatigue and conscious control interact to harm performance, we should see a larger pressure dip (say, −5 pp late-close) in high-workload situations and a smaller dip in low-workload situations.

**Finding:** Across 519,640 NBA free throws (2015-24), no such interaction exists. Six tests of workload×pressure yielded:
- Clutch × top-quartile season minutes: p = .55
- Clutch × back-to-back: p = .85
- Clutch × in-game minutes: p = .43
- Clutch × prior-7-day minutes: p = .16
- Playoffs × high workload: p = .72
- Late-close × back-to-back: p = .09 (suggestive, but CI spans zero)

The absence of an interaction does not prove the cascade is false, because:
1. Outcome studies can't measure the intermediate mechanisms (kinematics, reinvestment propensity).
2. Selection confounding (coaches playing hot players in close games) masks fatigue effects.
3. Fatigue may alter mechanics without affecting outcome.

But it does place a constraint: **any proposed fatigue-reinvestment mechanism must be compatible with the null outcome interaction we observe.**

## A More Precise Hypothesis

The evidence allows for a more limited claim: In NBA free throws, late-minute pressure reduces performance (−2.5 pp, larger when trailing), replicating prior studies (Cao et al. 2011; Toma 2017). This dip is independent of measured workload (back-to-backs, minutes played). Whether the dip is mediated by conscious control, anxiety, attentional narrowing, or some other psychological mechanism remains unmeasured. A pre-registered study pairing MSRS/DSRS scores with free-throw kinematics and outcome (across rested, high-workload, and pressure conditions) would close this gap.
