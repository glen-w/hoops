# MEDIA chapter draft: Rights and visibility, 1990–2026

## Narrative arc
The NBA's media landscape transformed from a single national broadcaster (NBC, 1990–2002) through the cable era (ESPN/TNT, 2002–2024) to a three-partner streaming-first tier (2025–36). Alongside this national consolidation, regional sports networks—the mechanism by which local teams built their own revenue streams—collapsed under debt and cord-cutting. The result is a league with rising national payouts ($6.9B per year by 2025, up from $150M in 1990) but fragmented local access and structural opacity around streaming pricing.

## National rights and the cable turn (1990–2024)

The first deal was NBC's 1989 capture of the national package for the 1990–94 season at $600M over four years—a leap from CBS's prior $176M deal [NYT, 1989; LA Times, 1989]. Turner (TNT/TBS) ran the cable side at roughly $275M for the same period. By 1997–98, the economics had shifted: NBC was paying $750M for a four-year extension, and Turner $890M. In 2002, NBC exited after 12 seasons. The league handed the national package to ESPN and ABC for $2.4B over six years, and Turner retained $2.2B, with games moving substantially to cable [NYT, 2002; ESPN, 2002]. This was the moment cable became the standard platform.

The cable deal extended in 2008 as an eight-year bundle at $7.4B (about $930M annually)—the first to explicitly include digital, broadband and mobile rights [Reuters, 2007; NYT, 2007]. By 2014, the nine-year deal reached $24B ($2.6B a year), split roughly between ESPN ($1.4B) and Turner ($1.2B) [SBJ, 2014]. The league release that year disclosed no dollar amounts; the figures come from the sports press.

On 24 July 2024, the league confirmed 11-year deals (2025-26 through 2035-36) with Disney (ESPN/ABC), NBCUniversal (NBC/Peacock) and Amazon Prime Video. Press reports assigned roughly $2.6B to Disney, $2.5B to NBC and $1.8B to Amazon—totaling ~$76–77B over the term [SBJ, 2024; Reuters, 2024]. The league has not published these figures. Warner Bros. Discovery (TNT's parent) attempted to match Amazon's offer, failed, sued in New York state court, and on 18 November 2024 settled with a new 11-year deal for international live rights (Nordics, Poland, Latin America) plus highlight and content rights. Inside the NBA—the iconic analysis show—will air on ESPN/ABC starting in 2025-26, produced by TNT Sports.

## The streaming tier and streaming opacity

The 2024-25 season marked a transition year. ESPN+ simulcast ABC games and the NBA Cup under the existing ESPN agreement. On 1 October 2025, the NBA took NBA TV operations back in-house, now airing about 60 games. Amazon Prime Video's national package begins in 2025-26 with 66 games plus the NBA Cup and Play-In.

Pricing for the consumer remains opaque. ESPN+ pricing is bundled into multi-tier Disney packages. The $11-per-month ESPN+ launched as a direct-to-consumer service on 21 August 2025, but the NBA-specific allocation is unknown. Peacock pricing for NBA content is not yet public. These arrangements violate the principle that consumers can see what they pay for a product—and make it impossible to measure cord-cutting effects or streaming viability. The undisclosed pricing prevents quantification of whether standalone streaming has emerged as a viable alternate to cable bundles.

## Regional sports networks: rise and collapse (2019–2026)

Regional sports networks (RSNs) were cable channels owned by or contracted to individual teams or groups of teams, airing local games, pre-game and post-game analysis, and local advertising. They made up a significant share of team revenue, especially for franchises in large media markets.

In August 2019, Sinclair Broadcast Group completed its $10.6B acquisition of 21 former Disney RSNs, rebranding them as Bally Sports in 2021 [Sinclair 8-K, 2019]. The consolidation seemed stable until cord-cutting accelerated. On 14 March 2023, Diamond Sports Group—the operating company for the Bally networks covering 19 networks, 42 teams and 16 NBA teams—filed for Chapter 11 with $8B in debt [Business Wire, 2023; AP, 2023]. The bankruptcy voided several team agreements; the first NBA teams to leave RSNs were the Suns (moved to Gray TV and Kiswe streaming, July 2023), the Jazz (KJZZ and Jazz+ app) and the Rockets (who acquired AT&T SportsNet Southwest, rebranding it as Space City Home Network, October 2023).

In May 2024, a carriage dispute between Comcast and Bally RSNs took the networks off Comcast platforms—affecting mostly MLB but hitting the end of the NBA playoffs [CNBC, 2024; resolution Business Wire, 2024]. Resolution came in July 2024, but the damage to the RSN model was irreparable. For 2024-25, Diamond dropped the Mavericks and Pelicans, retaining 13 NBA teams. The Mavericks moved to TEGNA (WFAA/KFAA) plus MavsTV+; the Pelicans went to Gray Media and Pelicans+; the Trail Blazers left Root Sports for Sinclair's over-the-air broadcast partnerships.

On 21 October 2024, Bally Sports was rebranded FanDuel Sports Network. Diamond emerged from bankruptcy on 2 January 2025 as Main Street Sports Group, managing 16 FDSN networks and 13 NBA teams [Business Wire, 2025]. In April 2026, Main Street wound down. NBA coverage ended 12 April 2026; Main Street paid no rights fees that year, and teams may recover about 60% of their prior revenue [SBJ, 2026]. The 13 affected teams (Hawks, Hornets, Cavaliers, Pistons, Pacers, Clippers, Grizzlies, Heat, Bucks, Timberwolves, Thunder, Magic, Spurs) became free to sign new deals for 2026-27.

For the 2026-27 season, the fragmented replacement was evident. DAZN (a streaming service owned by Perform Group) took exclusive deals for the Pacers, Cavaliers, Timberwolves, Grizzlies and Spurs, each simulcast over the air in limited quantities. Other teams used hybrid models: over-the-air TV plus team apps or Prime Video. A few—Celtics, Warriors, 76ers, Kings, Nets, Knicks, Nuggets, Lakers, Bulls, Wizards, Rockets, Raptors—remain on traditional RSNs (NBC Sports regionals, YES, MSG, Altitude, Spectrum, CHSN, Monumental, Space City, TSN/Sportsnet). The league floated a national "local hub" deal with YouTube at $1B+ per year for 20–25 teams, but as of October 2026 this remains unconfirmed reporting [SBJ/SportsMediaWatch, 2026].

## International stratification: Tencent and the China factor

In January 2015, Tencent signed a five-year digital deal (starting July 2015) reported at about $500M [NYT, 2015; ESPN, 2015]. In July 2019, Tencent expanded to a five-year term through 2024-25 at a reported $1.5B. In October 2019, following Daryl Morey's Hong Kong solidarity tweet, Tencent suspended NBA preseason streams [CNN, 2019]. State broadcaster CCTV kept the NBA off the air entirely until 10 October 2020 (Finals Game 5) [NBA news, 2020; SCMP, 2020]. League commissioner Adam Silver estimated the loss at "hundreds of millions"—likely under $400M [CNBC, 2020].

In October 2024, Tencent renewed as exclusive digital partner. Reports indicate a term through about 2027, but the value is not public. Whether the deal is smaller due to political risk remains unknown. The 2025 return of games to Chinese state TV suggests a partial normalization [Sina, 2024; QQ, 2024].

Tencent deals illustrate a third layer of rights stratification: international digital agreements that can be switched off by geopolitical pressure. Crawford et al. (2018) model the welfare effects of vertical integration in multichannel television markets, a framework applicable to cable distributors that own RSNs and thus control carriage. Hutchins, Li & Rowe (2019) theorize the streaming tier, including platforms like Amazon and DAZN. He & Tao (2023) analyze the 2019 Morey/China episode as a cross-national conflict.

## Sponsorship landscape (2024-25)

Arena naming rights totaled $175M for State Farm (Hawks), $119.1M for TD Bank (Celtics), and reported amounts for about 17 of 30 venues. Jersey patch sponsorships are even more opaque: only the Lakers–Bibigo deal ($100M+ over five years, LA Times 2021) is properly reported. Hornets, Clippers and Spurs had no patch sponsor at the start of 2024-25. The 30-team inventory exists, but 13 arena values and 29 patch values remain undisclosed or estimated. The Blinkfire and GlobalData records capture what is publicly announced; no proprietary database aggregates the full sponsorship landscape.

## Viewership and the local effect

Foster et al. (2014) measure drivers of local NBA TV ratings; Mongeon & Winfree (2012) compare television and gate demand. Salaga, Tainsky & Mondello (2020) link betting market outcomes to viewership. Kunz (2017) documents the RSN boom written before the collapse.

## Remaining gaps

- Season-by-season fee escalators (how payments rose annually within each contract term) do not appear to be public.
- The 2024 reported annual values ($2.6B, $2.5B, $1.8B) are press-reported; the NBA has never confirmed them.
- Tencent's 2024 renewal value is unknown, and the end season is uncertain (2026-27 or 2027-28).
- YouTube's reported $1B+ local-hub deal is unconfirmed.
- ESPN+, Peacock and Tencent pricing for NBA content are not publicly itemized—a structural opacity that prevents measurement of streaming viability.
- A causal study of NBA blackouts specifically (the impact of games not televised in local markets) has not been found in peer-reviewed literature.
