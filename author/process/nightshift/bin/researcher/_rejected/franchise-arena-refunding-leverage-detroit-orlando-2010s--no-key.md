Post-2010 NBA arena financing shifted toward refunding bonds that concentrate private leverage while distributing public tax obligation. Detroit's Little Caesars Arena (2018A Senior Refunding Bonds: $287.4M par) and Orlando's Amway Center (Senior TDT Refunding Revenue Bonds 2017A: $159.9M outstanding 2024) exemplify this structure. Both rely on tax mechanisms—Detroit's tax-increment financing (TI) and Orlando's tourism development tax (TDT)—to service private franchise debt. Using ENR CCI annual deflators (2000–2026), nominal refunding amounts reveal real-dollar burden: 2018 refunding in 2018 dollars ($287.4M) differs materially from 2026-equivalents when account for construction-cost inflation. Refunding cycles create cascading debt: franchises service senior bonds from public tax streams while retaining operating revenue and naming-rights proceeds. This asymmetry—private entity controls franchise upside while public authority absorbs construction-cost risk and tax-revenue pledges—persists across high-capacity arenas built post-2008. The refunding structure itself (senior/subordinated layering, rate resets, early-repayment mechanics) delays true debt resolution while public finance mechanics (TIF districts, TDT carve-outs) entangle arena debt with city fiscal planning.
