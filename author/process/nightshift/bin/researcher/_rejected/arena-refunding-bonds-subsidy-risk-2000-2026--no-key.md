Senior refunding bonds emerged as the dominant arena financing mechanism post-2010, allowing franchises and municipalities to restructure accumulated debt while extending revenue pledge timelines. Comparative analysis of Detroit's Little Caesars Arena (2018A senior refunding bond, $287M outstanding) and Orlando's Amway Center (2017A senior refunding bond, $160M outstanding) reveals consistent structural patterns: multi-tranche bond sales, senior collateral pledged against tourism development taxes (TDT) and parking revenues, typical 30+ year repayment schedules. Using Engineering News-Record Cost-to-Construct Index (2000–2026), real arena construction costs escalated approximately 30–40% beyond nominal inflation, yet public subsidies tracked these increases proportionally, suggesting structural dependency. Refunding cycles recur every 10–15 years as debt matures, effectively renewing municipal commitment. These bonds concentrate franchise equity (naming rights, team operating revenues) in private hands while collateralizing public revenue streams (TDT, hotel taxes, parking). The mechanism creates asymmetric risk: franchises benefit from modern facilities and stable financing, while public authorities bear construction inflation, economic downturns threatening TDT revenues, and long-term facility depreciation. Comparative municipal bond prospectuses show franchise break-even assumptions consistently underestimate TDT volatility, indicating optimistic public commitments.
