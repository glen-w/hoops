Little Caesars Arena (Detroit) and Amway Center (Orlando) underwent senior refunding bond issuances in 2017–2018 with starkly different public debt burdens. Detroit's 2018A refunding bonds carried $287.4M in par value; Orlando's 2017A refunding bonds totaled $159.9M. Both arenas employed tax-backed mechanisms—Detroit via Tax-Increment Financing (TIF), Orlando via Tourism Development Tax (TDT)—yet the scale disparity reveals subsidy asymmetry.

Normalized via ENR CCI deflators across 2000–2026, Detroit's $287M par in 2018 represented proportionally steeper public obligation than Orlando's $160M in 2017. Public risk distribution diverged fundamentally: Detroit's TIF model concentrated development-tax capture within a narrow district boundary, creating acute revenue dependency and default exposure; Orlando's TDT diffused hotel-tax collection across county-wide hospitality sectors, spreading subsidy burden but obscuring accountability for arena-specific support.

Neither arena required explicit annual operational subsidy. Yet 20–30-year bond amortization schedules lock public budgets into arena-dedicated debt service decades beyond franchise viability windows, crowding out reinvestment in schools, transit, and workforce development. The contrast illustrates how subsidy timing and tax mechanism shape long-term public fiscal constraint.
