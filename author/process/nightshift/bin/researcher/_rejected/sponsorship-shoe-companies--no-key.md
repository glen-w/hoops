Corporate sponsorship emerged as a central revenue stream and representational mechanism in professional basketball from the 1960s onwards, with shoe and equipment manufacturers playing a pivotal role. Converse's All-Star sneaker, established in 1917 and endorsed by Chuck Taylor from 1918, dominated the early market until Nike's aggressive entry in the 1980s with Michael Jordan as its marquee endorsement (Smart 2007). This shift marked a turning point: Varea-Calero et al. (2025) define sponsorship as "the financial, material and human support and collaboration between a company and a sports entity," encompassing not merely promotion but the establishment of consumer connection, brand loyalty, and sales objectives. Thomas (2021) documents how the NBA, from 1984 to 2005, subordinated representational strategies to "corporate colonialism," allowing sneaker, merchandise, and sportswear producers to shape the league's portrayal of franchise players. Yet scholarly debate persists about whether commercial objectives should override "the true spirit of sport" (Gammelsæter 2021, cited in Varea-Calero et al. 2025). The symbiosis between shoe companies and professional basketball fundamentally transformed both the sport's cultural representation and its financial structure during this period.
