Regional and local television broadcasting shaped NBA franchise economics from the 1960s onward through a dual revenue model. The NBA centralised national broadcast contracts—worth $180 million annually by 1990–91, with revenue distributed equally amongst all 27 teams—whilst individual teams retained 100% of revenues from local broadcasts of games not covered nationally (Bell, n.d.). This hybrid structure incentivised teams to develop local media markets aggressively. However, since the early 1980s the League imposed operational constraints: a Board of Governors resolution capped each team at 41 games (half the regular season) on free, over-the-air broadcast stations, limiting market saturation (Bell, n.d.). Regional networks expanded within this framework. Teams like the Bulls switched to superstation partners such as WGN-TV, whose cable reach transformed local markets into quasi-national distribution channels, generating substantial additional revenue beyond traditional broadcast fees (Bell, n.d.). By 1990, national and local broadcast income constituted approximately 80% of all team revenues, making broadcasting rights central to franchise valuation, expansion viability, and relocation economics. Local market size, broadcasting infrastructure, and regional network partnerships thus became critical factors in strategic franchise decisions throughout the period.
