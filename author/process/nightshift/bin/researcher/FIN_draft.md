# FIN chapter draft: Who paid for the buildings, 2010–2026

## Narrative arc
Since 2010, NBA arenas have been financed in radically different ways. Two Bay Area and Los Angeles buildings were privately built: Chase Center ($1.4B, 2019) and Intuit Dome ($1.8–2B, 2024). Sacramento (Golden 1, $558M, 46% public), Milwaukee (Fiserv Forum, $524M, 48% public) and Detroit (Little Caesars, $863M, 38% public) funded their arenas through city bonds and tax-increment schemes. Brooklyn's Barclays Center ($1.07B, 2012) used tax-exempt bonds repaid from payments in lieu of property taxes, making its "public share" ambiguous depending on how you count tax instruments.

The consensus across 130+ peer-reviewed studies is that such venues bring little or no local economic growth. When omitted costs—land, surrounding infrastructure, forgone property taxes—are counted, the average public subsidy rises about 40% above headline figures. The most recent arena, Intuit Dome, was privately built by Steve Ballmer's Clippers but sits on city land transferred at terms not disclosed, leaving its true public cost unknown.

## The private buildouts (2019, 2024)

Chase Center (Golden State Warriors, 2019) cost approximately $1.4B and was entirely privately financed by the ownership group and their investors. The site, on Mission Bay in San Francisco, is also a development district that benefited from surrounding public infrastructure (waterfront improvements, transit), but the cost of those investments was not found in public sources.

Intuit Dome (Los Angeles Clippers, 2024) opened at an estimated $1.8–2B cost and was privately financed by Ballmer's ownership. The team paid a reported $100M in community benefits. The building sits on land sold or transferred to the Clippers by the City of Los Angeles; the terms of that land deal have not been publicly reported. This gap—the value and conditions of the city's contribution—makes the "privately financed" classification incomplete.

## The subsidized arenas (2016–2018)

Golden 1 Center (Sacramento Kings, 2016) cost $558.2M. The city financed $255M (45.7%) through lease-revenue bonds backed by parking revenue, then captured in a lease agreement and tax increment. The public share is well documented.

Fiserv Forum (Milwaukee Bucks, 2018) cost $524M. The state contributed $80M; the city and county used tax-increment finance bonds through the Wisconsin Center District. The combined public share was $250M (47.7%).

Little Caesars Arena (Detroit Red Wings, 2017) cost $862.9M and was funded with $324M in public money (37.5%) through Detroit Development Authority tax-increment bonds that captured school taxes during and after the city's bankruptcy. This was a particularly contentious subsidy: schools had been cut during the bankruptcy, and a new arena was partly funded by redirecting school property tax growth (Sroka 2024).

## The Barclays trap (Brooklyn, 2012)

Barclays Center cost approximately $1.07B. Direct city and state capital was $200–231M (reported by the NYC Independent Budget Office and Institutional Investor). But $511M came from tax-exempt PILOT bonds (payments in lieu of property tax), repaid from payments made *instead* of property taxes. The center also received property-tax exemptions, sales-tax exemptions and mortgage-recording-tax exemptions, plus below-market land from the MTA. Whether you count PILOT bonds as "public money" changes the answer completely. The NYC Independent Budget Office itself—New York's own fiscal watchdog—said these subsidies are hard to compare across cities. A straightforward percentage is misleading. The chapter can describe the instruments without committing to a single figure.

## The economics consensus

Bradbury, Coates & Humphreys (2023) reviewed 130+ studies of sports venue subsidies in the US, Canada and Europe. Consensus: very limited local economic impact. Subsidies are not justified by growth, and any claimed returns are typically offset by opportunity costs. Long (2005) showed that when you add omitted costs—land, infrastructure, forgone taxes—the average public subsidy rises from a headline average of $126M to $177M (+40%). His method is now standard and predates these new arenas, but the principle holds: headline figures understate the true public cost.

Propheter (2012) tested whether arenas "spur local development" in the NBA—a method applicable across stadiums. Swindell & Rosentraub (1998) surveyed who benefits from teams and what that implies for public policy. These studies predate the 2010 arenas, but their conclusions hold: the consensus is that venues do not generate the local growth used to justify public investment.

## Where the evidence gaps are

- **Barclays public share:** depends on PILOT counting. No agreed figure exists; the NYC IBO recommends describing the instruments instead.
- **Chase Center surrounding infrastructure:** the cost of waterfront development and transit is not quantified.
- **Intuit Dome city land value:** the terms under which LA transferred or sold land to the Clippers are not public.
- **SoFi Stadium (NFL, 2020):** nothing sourced; kept out of the table.
- **Crypto.com Arena renovation costs (2022+):** not found.

## What the book can safely say

Since 2010, NBA arenas have been paid for in very different ways. Chase Center and Intuit Dome were privately financed, though Intuit Dome sits on city land transferred at undisclosed terms. Sacramento, Milwaukee and Detroit each put roughly 38–48% public money into their new arenas, mostly through city bonds and tax-increment schemes—with the understanding that such venues, according to the research consensus of 130+ studies, bring little new local economic growth. Brooklyn's Barclays Center used tax-exempt bonds repaid from payments in lieu of property taxes, a mechanism that makes its "public share" a matter of definition. Headline subsidy totals usually leave out land, infrastructure and forgone taxes—adding these omitted costs typically raises the average public share by about 40%.

## Chapter maturity estimate
~35% complete. The post-2010 arena financing is sourced and detailed for 5 NBA buildings. The consensus on economic impact is anchored to Bradbury et al. (2023) and Long (2005). The gaps (Barclays percentage, infrastructure costs for Chase and Intuit, Intuit's land deal) are named. The chapter lacks narrative about *why* cities continued to subsidize during a period when research showed no economic return—a cultural and political question beyond the scope of financial accounting alone, and one the book may not answer.
