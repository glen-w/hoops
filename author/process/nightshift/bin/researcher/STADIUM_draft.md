# STADIUM chapter draft: Arenas and neighborhoods, gentrification without displacement studies, COVID as experiment

## Narrative arc
New arenas change their neighborhoods, almost always for the better from a property-value perspective: nearby homes and shops gain value, often before the arena opens. That rising value is the engine of gentrification worries. But no study has used causal methods to measure whether an NBA arena actually pushed residents out. Sacramento's Golden 1 brought price gains nearby, yet existing shops survived 53% shorter on average, and police response times slowed on game nights. Edmonton's Rogers Place (an NHL arena) involved interviews with residents about displacement and "carceral redlining"—economic and security pressures on the area's lowest-income and Indigenous residents. When COVID emptied NBA arenas in 2020, home-court advantage fell from 2.1 points per game to about 0.4 points, a drop attributable primarily to player effort rather than referee bias. The gap between price effects (well-measured) and displacement (plausible, partially documented, but not yet causally measured for any NBA arena) is the largest single hole in the book.

## Price effects near new arenas (the evidence base)

Propheter (2019) studied Barclays Center in Brooklyn. Using a triple difference-in-differences design on commercial parcels within one mile (2006–15), he found that net operating income per square foot rose by about $2.71/sqft per 1,000 feet of distance from the arena. The effect was local—fading with distance—and substantial: the closest commercial properties to the arena gained real value.

Keeler, Stephens & Humphreys (2021) studied Staples Center in Los Angeles. Using spatial difference-in-differences, they found that nearby property values rose, with anticipation effects—prices moving up *before* the arena opened.

Chikish, Humphreys & Nowak (2019) studied Oklahoma City's arena and Thunder arrival using hedonic pricing and event timing. Prices rose after the new arena and a permanent NBA team arrived. A temporary NBA team also had a positive effect.

More broadly, Feng & Humphreys (2012) analyzed facilities across all four major leagues and found positive value effects that decay with distance, fading by about five miles. Huang & Humphreys (2014) used mortgage data across 56 new facilities and found roughly 20% more mortgage applications nearby, with over half explained by the location advantage alone.

These studies agree: proximity to a new arena raises property values, often substantially and locally.

## The Sacramento paradox (Golden 1, 2016)

Sacramento's Golden 1 Center presents the complexity of the gentrification story. Propheter (2020a) compared police response times on game nights versus non-game nights using an event-day methodology: nearby response times were 7.4% (~33 seconds) slower during events, a hidden social cost.

More strikingly, Propheter (2020b) used survival analysis on retail establishments within a half-mile of the arena. Existing retail that was in place before the arena opened survived about 53% shorter than would be expected—meaning the nearby shops turned over much faster after the arena arrived, a form of business displacement.

Propheter (2020c) used localized synthetic control to test whether the arena sparked a building boom. It did not: there was no significant effect on building permits.

The Sacramento story, then, is price appreciation in nearby property, faster turnover and closure of existing retail, slower police response on game nights, and no development boom. The displacement of businesses is measurable; the displacement of residents remains unstudied.

## The Edmonton qualitative evidence (Rogers Place, NHL, not NBA)

Rogers Place opened in Edmonton in 2016 as an NHL arena (not NBA). Scherer, Davidson, Kafara & Koch (2021) conducted a two-year ethnography of the surrounding ICE District. They documented how lower-income, unhoused and Indigenous residents experienced the neighborhood's transformation through "carceral redlining"—increased police presence, private security, and the selective enforcement of rules that excluded them from the public space that the arena and district had become. Scherer et al. (2025) expanded this work in a community-based 62-interview study, framing "sport-related gentrification" as a process that operates alongside and through settler colonialism. Evans, Collins & Chai (2019) examined how downtown service hubs coped as the arena district grew. Scherer (2016) documented community opposition to the CAD $606.5M arena subsidy.

These are qualitative studies—ethnography and interviews—not causal estimates. They document displacement and exclusion through resident experience. They have not been replicated for any NBA arena.

## Detroit (Little Caesars, 2017): the gap

Sroka (2024) studied Little Caesars Arena financing as a subsidy case study. The arena received $324M in public funding through Detroit Development Authority tax-increment bonds, capturing school tax growth during and after Detroit's bankruptcy. The subsidy story is documented. The displacement story is not. Only grey literature exists: a CLOSUP student working paper and a Michigan thesis on 15 arenas. No causal study of resident displacement in the District Detroit area has been published.

## Teams as disamenity: Seattle and Charlotte

Humphreys & Nowak (2017) studied the opposite question: what happens when a team leaves? Using difference-in-differences on departure timing, they found that nearby home prices *rose* by 6–7% in Seattle (condos) and 7.5–14% in Charlotte after the NBA teams left. The teams themselves were *disamenities*—their presence suppressed values. This result inverts the usual gentrification story and suggests that the value premium of a new arena may come partly from factors other than the team's on-court presence.

## The COVID natural experiment (2020): home advantage without fans

When COVID-19 closed arenas to fans in March 2020, the NBA had a rare natural experiment: same players, same rules, same court, but no fans. Ganz & Allsop (2023) used panel fixed effects and instrumental variables (attendance caps as an instrument) to estimate the fan effect. They found home advantage dropped from 2.13 points per game (with fans, prior six seasons) to 0.44 points without fans—a loss of about 1.7 points per 1,000 fans. The effect was substantial.

But crucially, they found that attendance changes *did not* significantly affect the foul margin. The fan effect was not running through referees. Gong (2022) tested referee bias directly using the NBA's own Last Two Minute Reports, which record all calls in close games. He found no significant crowd-caused home bias in close-game calls. Some specific missed-call types shifted about 2.3–2.4%, but this was not the large systematic bias one might expect.

Ehrlich & Potter (2023) used attendance variation in 2020-21 games and found empty arenas erased home advantage, while small crowds kept it. But NBA officials were not measurably influenced by crowd size. Leota et al. (2022) used mediation analysis on 1,080 NBA games and found home win probability dropped from 58.65% with crowds to 50.60% without. They tested whether rebounding (effort) mediated the effect and found it did—the mechanism was player effort, not referee bias.

Price, Remer & Stone (2012) provide pre-COVID evidence of referee favoritism in a different direction: they find home teams, and teams trailing in a game or series, get more of the judgment-call turnovers—a pattern consistent with discretionary bias rather than the open-court home advantage effect.

The consensus is clear: the absence of fans reduces home advantage substantially, but the effect works through the players (effort, rebounding, morale) rather than through biased refereeing. Soccer tells a different story: Pettersson-Lidbom & Priks (2010), Bryson et al. (2021) and Scoppa (2021) show empty stadiums made referees fairer to away teams in European soccer, a mechanism not seen in basketball.

## Technical-foul home advantage: untested via COVID

The Ganz & Allsop (2023) study tested scoring and fouls broadly. Technical fouls are the highest-discretion calls a referee makes—there is no objective rule for what warrants a technical. If referees favor home teams in judgment-call situations, technical fouls should be the clearest test. The COVID-era data exist in league records. No published study has yet tested whether empty arenas reduced the home-team technical-foul advantage. This is noted as researchable and high-priority.

## Other basketball contexts (EuroLeague and NCAA)

Paulauskas et al. (2022) studied EuroLeague before and after COVID. Home advantage fell from about 70% to about 51%, and free-throw-attempt advantage changed. In Spain's ACB, fouls shifted in some cases. Jiménez, Valido & Jiménez (2025) compared EuroLeague and ACB ghost-game periods and found mixed effects: home win probability fell 5.6–5.8%, but the foul effect varied by league. Marshall, Locke & Wooten (2025) studied NCAA 2020–21 and found less attendance reduced home advantage, more so in power conferences.

## What the book can safely say

Studies that compare places near new arenas with places farther away usually find nearby property values going up, often before the doors open. That is the economic engine of gentrification. But no study has yet shown, with a causal design, that an NBA arena pushed residents out. Sacramento's new arena brought shorter lives for existing nearby shops, slower police response on game nights and no building boom. In Edmonton—an NHL arena, not NBA—researchers who interviewed residents describe a "sport-related gentrification" whose costs fell unevenly, especially on the area's Indigenous urban street community and less affluent residents. Price rises are well established; displacement is plausible and documented in qualitative work, but it has not been measured with causal methods for any NBA arena.

When COVID emptied NBA arenas, home-court advantage almost vanished: from about two points a game to under half a point. The best estimate is that every 1,000 fans add roughly 1.7 points. But when researchers looked at the referees' calls, including the league's own Last Two Minute Reports, they found little sign that NBA officials had been tilting calls toward the home team because of the crowd. The fans seem to work mainly through the players. Soccer is different: there, empty stadiums clearly made referees fairer to visitors.
