The NBA became the first major American professional sports league to implement a salary cap, introduced through the 1983 collective bargaining agreement and taking effect in the 1984–85 season. The cap's origins lay in league owners' response to escalating player salaries and perceived competitive imbalance stemming from disparate local revenue streams—whilst national television revenues were shared equally amongst teams, local gate receipts and local broadcast rights created wide variations in spending capacity {FFCFKPJ5}.

The original salary cap was structured to limit team payrolls to 53–57 per cent of the league's gross basketball-related income (BRI), representing a negotiated compromise that granted players a guaranteed revenue share in exchange for accepting a ceiling on aggregate team expenditure. This arrangement, characterised as "communitarian capitalism," reflected the strong role of the league commissioner and the commitment of both management and labour to stabilise the NBA's finances after years of instability {X7L76G7E}.

The basic cap structure proved remarkably durable, surviving collective bargaining negotiations in 1988 and 1995 despite threats of lockouts and antitrust challenges. The NFL did not adopt its cap until 1994, and the NHL not until 2005–06, establishing the NBA as an early innovator in sports labour regulation {FFCFKPJ5}. The salary cap became foundational to the league's competitive model, though its effectiveness in promoting competitive balance remained contested amongst economists.
