# Fatigue, Workload, and Psychological Pressure in Free-Throw Performance

## Fatigue Effects on Mechanics: Evidence from Small Samples

Research on fatigue-induced changes to free-throw kinematics comes primarily from controlled lab and small-sample field studies.

**Wrist mechanics under induced fatigue:** Rusdiana et al. (2020) tested 10 high-school players (aged 16–18) on free-throw kinematics before and after a Repeated Anaerobic Sprint Test (RAST, designed to induce fatigue). Among eight kinematic indicators, only wrist angular velocity showed a significant relationship to free-throw outcome under fatigue [6REBX2D3]. The effect was correlational and measured via 2D video (Kinovea), and the sample size is small enough that replication is essential. Rusdiana reported no measure of kinematic *variability* and no psychological measure (reinvestment scale, self-report anxiety), so the results do not support claims linking fatigue to shot consistency or conscious control.

**Angle invariance under fatigue:** In contrast, Uygur et al. (2010) tested 10 collegiate players on free-throw joint angles under both rested and fatigued conditions (induced via 15-minute treadmill run). They found no significant fatigue effect on shoulder, elbow, wrist, knee or ankle angles, or on free-throw accuracy [cited in researcher-05 report, pending full retrieval]. This null result suggests that at least for joint angles, fatigue tolerance in the upper body is high in trained players.

**Summary:** Fatigue's effect on free-throw mechanics remains incompletely understood. The positive evidence (wrist angular velocity) comes from n = 10; the null evidence also comes from n = 10. Neither is large enough to claim causality, and neither measured the trait variables (reinvestment scales, anxiety propensity) that would link mechanics to player psychology.

## Reinvestment and Conscious Control: A Hypothesis Without Basketball Evidence

The theory of reinvestment proposes that under pressure, some players revert to conscious, step-by-step control of their movements, disrupting automaticity. If true, we would expect high-reinvestment players to show more kinematic variability under pressure and worse outcome performance. **No basketball study has tested this prediction.**

**Reinvestment scales and their scope:** The Movement-Specific Reinvestment Scale (MSRS, Masters & Maxwell 2008) and Decision-Specific Reinvestment Scale (DSRS, Kinrade et al. 2010) measure trait propensities for conscious self-monitoring and rumination. MSRS was developed and validated on golf putting, field-hockey dribbling, and soccer skills. Kinrade et al.'s DSRS was tested on a computer-based basketball *passing-decision* task (not shooting); the rumination subscale was the stronger predictor. Neither scale has been paired with basketball free-throw kinematics in the same cohort.

**Evidence from golf: opposite sign.** The closest direct test comes from Malhotra et al. (2015), who measured MSRS scores in 30 novice golfers and correlated them with kinematic variability (SD of impact velocity and face angle) during putting practice. The result was the reverse of the reinvestment hypothesis: **higher MSRS scores went with *lower* kinematic variability**, and movement self-consciousness was positively correlated with putting proficiency. A chapter claiming "high reinvesters move more variably" must cite this as a counter-example.

**Other kinematic evidence:** A broader literature on conscious processing and motor control finds mixed results on the direction of variability change. Toner & Moran (2011) showed that technical-adjustment instructions reduced stroke *consistency* in expert golfers without hurting holing rate, a dissociation between conscious *monitoring* (hurt putting) and conscious *adjustment* (hurt consistency but not outcome). Gray (2004) found that a skill-focused dual task raised movement variability in *experts* only, suggesting that skill level moderates the effect. Van Ginneken et al. (2018) found that high propensity for conscious control correlated with *reduced* throwing-arm variability in learners. Taken together, these studies suggest that conscious control and variability are moderated by expertise level and the type of attention instruction, not a single direction.

**Where the gap remains:** Basketball free-throw shooting under pressure has been outcome-tested (showing small pressure dips), but the mechanism—whether mediated by reinvestment, anxiety, automaticity loss, or other factors—has not been measured in the same study. Kinematics and reinvestment scales exist in separate literatures, and until both are measured in the same player cohort, the "cascade" hypothesis (fatigue → kinematic variability → reinvestment → performance decrement) remains a hypothesis.

## Workload and Choking: Outcome Data

At the outcome level, workload (back-to-back games, cumulative minutes, in-game minutes played) does not amplify pressure-induced performance drops. See GAME TIME / Free-Throw Pressure for details: no interaction between clutch situations and workload measures (all p > .05), and the main effects of workload go in the opposite direction (more minutes, slightly better FT%), likely due to selection (coaches playing the hot players in close games) rather than a protective effect of fatigue.

**Implication:** Fatigue may alter mechanics (wrist angle, release height) without harming outcomes, or the outcome improvement from selection is offsetting any fatigue cost. Direct measurement of fatigue—via heart-rate variability, blood lactate, or load-management data—would be needed to settle whether fatigue is protective, neutral, or harmful under the current testing regime.
