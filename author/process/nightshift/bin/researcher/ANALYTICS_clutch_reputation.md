# Clutch Reputation and the Noise Floor

The NBA celebrates clutch performers: players who are supposed to thrive in high-stakes moments. But at what skill level can we actually identify them?

We have outcome data on free throws in clutch situations—the highest-pressure attempts in basketball. Over 9 seasons (2015-16 to 2023-24), shooters faced roughly 24,000 clutch free throws outside the final minute, and 9,800 in the final minute of close games. These are high-stakes, low-frequency events for each player. A typical NBA free-throw shooter faces fewer than 50 clutch attempts per season.

**The problem is binomial noise.** With about 100 clutch attempts per player across the full period, the standard error of a percentage is roughly ±4 percentage points. True differences in clutch susceptibility would need to exceed this noise floor to be detectable. The data show that individual "choke gaps" (the difference between a player's baseline FT% and their clutch FT%) are not stable year-to-year (r = 0.08) and not predictable from the player's overall shooting skill (r = 0.10 with baseline FT%). In statistical terms, this is what noise looks like: random variation that doesn't persist and doesn't follow a pattern.

This doesn't mean clutch doesn't exist—the group-level effect is real (−2.5 pp in late-close situations)—but it means we can't reliably rank individual players by how much they choke. The clutch reputation, especially for a particular player, is mostly noise.

For the book, the uncommon insight is this: **Clutch performance is a real effect at the population level, but not a stable trait for individuals.** A player who made clutch free throws last season is not likelier than random chance to make them again this season. Their reputation may derive from a small run of good or bad luck, or from performance in non-clutch situations that we've mistaken for clutch skill.
