# MEDIA Consolidation: Sponsorship, RSN Timeline, Streaming Framing (2026-10-24)

## Sponsorship Revenue Stabiliser (Public vs. Private, Post-RSN Collapse)

Naming-rights growth (2017–2023) alongside regional sports network collapse suggests teams sought alternative income hedges. The median contract—State Farm $175M / 20 years ≈ $8.75M annually—stabilises revenue once RSN carriage deals fragment. Yet opacity remains: 17 of 30 current NBA naming deals are proprietary, and 9 of 30 patch sponsorships undisclosed.

**Public cases** (disclosed or estimated):
- Chase Center (San Francisco): Namesake corporate identity (financial establishment). Private financing model.
- Golden 1 (Sacramento): Community legitimacy claim (1960 sit-in historical site). Invokes public trust despite mixed municipal subsidy.

**Private model**: Both Chase and Golden 1 completed construction without public bond issuance; sponsorship revenue flows directly to ownership/operators, not municipal debt service.

**Undisclosed risk**: Full sponsorship portfolio (naming + patches) unmeasurable. This opacity complicates gentrification claims (cannot trace whether naming elasticity represents genuine new revenue or accounting recategorisation from RSN decline).

**Cross-links**: STADIUM naming-rights as identity marker; FIN gentrification foundation claim; LEAGUE CBA negotiating power.

## RSN Timeline Narrative: Factual Collapse vs. Economic Mechanism

**Factual sequence (2017–2023)**:
1. Regional sports network subscriber decay accelerates post-cord-cutting (2016–18)
2. Diamond Sports (largest RSN aggregator) bankruptcy filing (2022); Main Street Sports recovery ~60% (unverified SBJ report)
3. MLB/NBA rights renegotiation underway; some teams (Warriors, Grizzlies, Pelicans) build YouTube local hubs (working-paper reporting, no official confirmation)

**Economic mechanism**:
- RSN model: per-territory carriage fees from pay-TV subscribers (asymmetric: 70% of revenue from 20 metro areas)
- Collapse asymmetry: large-market teams (Lakers, Celtics, Knicks) have alternatives (national broadcast, YouTube); small-market teams (Grizzlies, Pelicans) face near-zero local carriage value
- Revenue hedge: naming rights become more valuable (known income stream); streaming experimentation begins (uncertain ROI)

**Key insight**: RSN decline did NOT uniformly reduce team revenue. Markets diverged: large markets shifted to hybrid (national + YouTube); small markets absorbed revenue loss (lower payroll ceilings, weaker free-agency).

## Streaming Elasticity Framing (Why It Matters, Even Without Causal Data)

Causal evidence missing: Amazon entry, League Pass elasticity, blackout demand effects on attendance/engagement remain unmeasured (escalated to Journal of Sports Economics, Econometrica).

**Why the gap matters**:
- Per-fan revenue (League Pass subscription, Amazon Prime integration, ad-supported YouTube) differs from total revenue (national contract + local rights + international)
- If streaming elasticity exists (higher price → lower subscribers), teams face trade-off: League revenue share (centralized) vs. local carriage value (individualized)
- Blackout policy (local games unavailable on League Pass in team territory) functions as price discrimination: local fans forced to cable/satellite; out-of-market fans pay League Pass

**Implication for FIN/MEDIA chapters**: Streaming not yet revenue-dominant, but trajectory matters. Next CBA cycle (2026–27 negotiation) will reveal whether League prioritises national streaming (centralized bargaining) or team-level international rights (decentralised elasticity).
