# FIN (Finance): A sourced draft (2026-10-23)

## Two ways to build an arena: public money and private money

Since 2010, NBA arenas have split into two distinct financing strategies. **Chase Center** (San Francisco, 2019) and **Intuit Dome** (Los Angeles, 2024) were privately financed. Owners and private investors covered the full $1.4B and ~$1.8–2B costs respectively. In contrast, **Golden 1 Center** (Sacramento, 2016), **Fiserv Forum** (Milwaukee, 2018) and **Little Caesars Arena** (Detroit, 2017) each used roughly 38–48% public money, raised through municipal bonds and tax-increment financing schemes. **Barclays Center** (Brooklyn, 2012) sits in between, using tax-exempt bonds repaid from payments in lieu of property taxes (PILOT bonds).

**Barclays is the accounting trap.** Its direct city and state capital was about $200–231M on a roughly $1.07B project—about 19–22% if only direct capital is counted. But $511M of the total came from tax-exempt PILOT bonds, which are repaid from money the owner pays *instead of* property taxes. Whether you count those as "public money" changes the answer completely. The NYC Independent Budget Office itself says such comparisons are difficult. The cleaner approach is to describe the *instruments* used (municipal capital, PILOT bonds, tax-exempt status, sales/mortgage-recording tax exemptions, below-market land conveyances) rather than quoting a single percentage.

**Golden 1 (Sacramento)** used roughly 46% public money through general obligation bonds, special tax increments and infrastructure investment. **Fiserv Forum (Milwaukee)** was about 48% public. **Little Caesars (Detroit)** was about 38% public through TIF (tax-increment financing) and state grants. All three experienced public debate over whether the public share was justified.

## What the economics literature says about sports subsidies

Across 130+ peer-reviewed studies, the consensus is stark: **new sports venues bring little or no local economic growth, so subsidies are rarely justified by growth arguments**. Researchers disagree on the magnitude of wasted subsidies, but not on the direction. A 2023 review (Bradbury, Coates & Humphreys) examined studies across 99 facilities; the variation was large, but all pointed the same way.

A second structural problem is that headline subsidy figures are usually understated. When studies add omitted costs—land, surrounding infrastructure, forgone property taxes—the average public subsidy rises about 40%. A $250M subsidy often masks a true public cost closer to $350M. Long (2005) documented this undercount across multiple cities.

## Arena neighborhoods: prices, shops, displacement and disamenity

When new arenas open near homes and commercial property, local **property values typically rise**, often in anticipation before opening. This is the economic mechanism behind gentrification concerns. Studies of **Staples Center** (Los Angeles, 2000), **Oklahoma City's Thunder arena** (2010), and **Barclays Center** (Brooklyn, 2012) all found positive price effects that fade with distance. Keeler et al. (2021, *Journal of Sports Economics*) found that Staples Center raised nearby prices with clear anticipation effects. Chikish, Humphreys & Nowak (2019, *Journal of Regional Analysis & Policy*) showed that Oklahoma City's permanent NBA team had a positive effect; even a temporary NBA team also raised prices. Propheter (2019) found that Barclays commercial property within a mile saw net operating income per square foot rise by about $2.71 on average, with closer properties seeing larger gains.

**But no study has yet causally shown that an NBA arena pushed people out.** The causal work measures prices, rents, permits and business survival, not residents moving. Displacement and "sport-related gentrification" are documented through interviews and ethnography, mainly in **Edmonton's Rogers Place** (ICE District, opened 2016). Community researchers (Scherer et al. 2021, 2025) found that less affluent residents and Indigenous street community members experienced the arena district's growth as a loss of familiar space and services. That qualitative evidence is important; it just is not causal evidence of outright displacement.

**Counterintuitively, when NBA teams *left* cities, nearby home prices *rose*.** Seattle Supersonics (2008) and Charlotte Bobcats (2002) departures led to price increases of 6–14%, so the teams themselves were disamenities to property owners (Humphreys & Nowak 2017). That fact complicates any narrative that teams always lift neighborhoods.

**Sacramento's Golden 1 (2016) offers a cautionary note.** Despite positive price effects nearby, the arena brought shorter average survival for existing nearby shops (about 53% shorter lifespans) and longer police response times in the arena area on game nights (about 7.4% slower, roughly 33 seconds). No building permit boom followed, suggesting that rising property values did not trigger new development. The arena created some local costs that do not show up in standard property-value studies.

## What the book can safely say

Since 2010, the methods for financing NBA arenas have diverged. The two most recent major buildings—Chase Center and Intuit Dome—were privately financed with no direct public subsidy. Older arenas in Sacramento, Milwaukee and Detroit each leaned on public bonds and tax-increment financing to cover roughly 40–48% of construction. Across 130+ economic studies, venues bring little new growth, so the public cost is rarely justified on economic grounds alone. When researchers compare places near new arenas with places farther away, they usually find nearby property values rising, often before opening. That price effect is the economic foundation of gentrification. Yet no causal study has shown an NBA arena pushed residents out; such displacement is documented in interviews and ethnographic research but not in statistical designs that can rule out confounding.

## References and gaps

The arena financing data rests on a mix of primary sources (city and county fiscal reports, SEC filings, arena press releases) and academic studies (Bradbury, Coates & Humphreys 2023 for the 130-study review; Long 2005 for the 40% undercount; Sroka 2024 for Detroit; Propheter papers for Barclays, Sacramento and crime; Keeler et al. 2021 for Staples Center; Chikish et al. 2019 for Oklahoma City; Humphreys & Nowak 2017 for departures). The Edmonton qualitative research (Scherer et al. 2025, 2021; Evans, Collins & Chai 2019; Scherer 2016) sits in sociology journals, not economics journals.

Missing or thin:
- **Causal evidence of resident displacement** at any of the four major arenas (Barclays, Golden 1, Fiserv, Little Caesars, or post-2010 generally)
- **Cost structures and public liability for player salaries** (effect on municipal budgets when salaries rise)
- **Long-term maintenance and renovation costs** for aging arenas and their public shares
- **Tax abatement duration and renewal**: what happens after the initial bond term (e.g., tax-exempt status lapsing, PILOT terms ending)
- **International comparators**: European arenas and Australian venues; public subsidy levels and outcomes overseas
- **Subleasing economics**: whether renting to minor-league teams, concerts or other events changes the public cost-benefit calculus
- **Intuit Dome outcomes**: post-opening impacts on surrounding Inglewood neighborhoods (the "Homes Not Domes" opposition documented but post-opening results unstudied)
