# MEDIA: A sourced draft (2026-10-23)

## The three tiers of rights

The NBA's media business runs on three stacked layers, each with its own economics [Media rights timeline].

At the top sits the **US national package**. NBC took it in 1990 at $600M over four years (about $150M annually), replacing CBS's prior deal of roughly $176M over four years. Turner's cable outlet (TNT/TBS) ran alongside at about $275M over those same four years. By 1997-98, NBC had renewed at $1.75B over four years while Turner climbed to $890M over four years. When the 2002 deal landed, NBC departed after 12 seasons. The new 6-year package split: ABC/ESPN paid $2.4B and Turner $2.2B for about $765M annually combined—the first major shift toward cable. In 2007, the rights bundled "digital, broadband and mobile" explicitly for the first time at $7.4B over eight years. By 2014–15, the $24B nine-year package had ESPN at roughly $1.4B annually and Turner at $1.2B.

In July 2024, the NBA reset the board with three partners: Disney (ESPN/ABC) at reported $2.6B a year, NBCUniversal (NBC/Peacock) at $2.5B, and Amazon Prime Video at $1.8B—totaling about $77B over 11 years. The league itself has not confirmed these figures; only the press has reported them. Game counts shifted dramatically: broadcast TV went from about 15 games to about 75 per season, and Amazon—entering the national package for the first time—took 66 games plus the NBA Cup and Play-In. Warner Bros. Discovery tried to match Amazon's terms but failed; in November 2024, WBD settled with a new international and content deal (highlight rights for Bleacher Report and House of Highlights) while *Inside the NBA* moved to ESPN/ABC beginning 2025-26, now produced by TNT Sports but no longer on its networks.

**Regional sports networks** (the middle tier) collapsed between 2023 and 2026. Sinclair purchased 21 former Fox RSNs from Disney in 2019 for $10.6B enterprise value and rebranded them as Bally Sports in 2021. In March 2023, Diamond Sports Group—which operated Bally across 19 regional entities covering 42 teams and 16 NBA teams—filed for Chapter 11 with over $8B in debt. By 2023-24, the Suns, Jazz and Rockets had fled to Gray Television, over-the-air broadcasters and team-owned streaming apps. Teams began leaving in waves: the Mavericks and Pelicans in 2024-25, followed by others. In January 2025, Diamond emerged from bankruptcy as Main Street Sports Group. By April 2026, Main Street wound down completely with no rights fees; teams could recover up to about 60% of lost local revenue while the NBA planned a national streaming "local hub" for 2027–28 at the earliest.

By the 2026-27 season, the regional map had scattered entirely. Five teams (Pacers, Cavaliers, Timberwolves, Grizzlies, Spurs) moved exclusively to DAZN, the Austrian subscription platform. Three teams paired DAZN with local over-the-air TV. Five more used over-the-air plus team apps or Prime Video. The rest clung to traditional RSN remnants (NBC Sports regionals, YES, MSG, Altitude, Spectrum SportsNet) or moved to team-operated streaming. For the first time since cable's rise in the 1980s, there was no single regional carrier for NBA basketball. That fragmentation left teams uncertain about their local revenue and fans uncertain where to find games.

**International digital** is the bottom layer, and it can vanish overnight. Tencent signed a five-year deal in 2015 for about $500M, renewed in 2019 at about $1.5B through 2024–25, then renewed again in October 2024 at an undisclosed price. In October 2019, after Daryl Morey's tweet about Hong Kong, Tencent suspended NBA preseason streams and state broadcaster CCTV kept NBA games off air until October 2020. Silver estimated the loss at "hundreds of millions" of dollars—probably under $400M. The deal is geopolitically fragile; it can be switched off for political reasons.

## Sponsorship: arena naming, patches, and what's not public

In 2024–25, the 30 NBA arenas carried **naming rights sponsors** worth between $1.8B and roughly $2B total over their remaining terms. Some values are public (State Farm's $175M over 20 years, TD Garden's $119.1M), others are closed. The deals cluster in the $100–200M range over 15–20 years, putting the annual average in the $8–15M band, though post-2010 inflation has pushed new deals higher.

Jersey **patch sponsorship** is newer, visible revenue. In 2024–25, 27 of 30 teams carried a patch. The Hornets, Clippers and Spurs had none at the start of the season. The only properly reported value is the Lakers' Bibigo deal at more than $100M over five years. GlobalData's league-wide estimate for patch sponsorship in 2024–25 was $285.8M—an average of about $10.6M per team—but that figure is an analyst estimate, not official. Other patches (Knicks–Experience Abu Dhabi at an estimated $30M, Nets–GetYourGuide at an estimated $20M) show up in media reports but rarely with confirmed amounts.

The hidden gap is that **most sponsorship values remain secret**. About 17 of 30 arena deals and most patch deals are not publicly disclosed. Teams and arena operators claim commercial sensitivity; they will not negotiate against publicly available numbers. Without a Sportico or SBJ subscription—both paywalled—the league's actual sponsorship floor is unknown.

## What the academic literature says

Peer-reviewed research on TV demand, ratings and network economics exists. Crawford et al. (2018, *Econometrica*) studied the welfare effects of vertical integration in cable markets and how RSN ownership affects carriage and prices. Foster et al. (2014, *Journal of Sport Management*) examined what drives regional NBA TV ratings. Kunz (2017, *Journal of Sports Media*) documented the RSN boom before its collapse. Mongeon & Winfree (2012, *Sport Management Review*) compared television demand with gate demand in the NBA. Hutchins, Li & Rowe (2019, *Media, Culture & Society*) provided theory for the streaming tier, including Amazon and DAZN. Salaga, Tainsky & Mondello (2020, *Journal of Sport Management*) found that close games and expected outcomes drive national NBA ratings. He & Tao (2023, *International Journal of Sport Communication*) analyzed the 2019 Morey/China controversy across countries.

However, no peer-reviewed study of the **causal effect of NBA blackouts** has been found. The closest parallel is NFL blackout literature, mostly in working papers. Academic literature on player/coach compensation tied to media exposure or viewership does not exist in the library.

## References and gaps

The media rights timeline rests on press reporting from the *New York Times*, *Reuters*, *Variety*, *Sports Business Journal* and trade press. Few figures come from the NBA itself; the league almost never publishes annual deal values. Several 1990s figures carry "medium confidence" because they come from secondary sources (a 1997 Variety recap, not a 1989 primary document). The Tencent 2024 renewal amount is undisclosed. The Main Street rebate (teams may recover up to about 60% of lost RSN revenue) comes from a single SBJ report and has not been independently verified.

Missing for a complete MEDIA chapter:
- **Causal studies of streaming tier demand and viewing behavior** (how Amazon's entry affected local blackout economics)
- **Player and coach compensation tied to media exposure or viewership metrics**
- **International broadcast negotiations outside China** (Europe, Latin America, India)
- **Economics of League Pass blackouts** and their impact on local demand
- **How scheduling affects media rights value** (Sunday night vs Friday afternoon games, rest patterns)
- **ESPN+ and Peacock pricing structures and subscriber data** for 2024-25 and beyond
- **YouTube's reported $1B+ annual local hub proposal** (currently unconfirmed reporting)
