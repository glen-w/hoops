# Ghost franchises

**Desk:** Glen’s Hoops — creative, cite-backed  
**Access date:** 2026-09-20 (Europe/Paris)  
**Rule:** Voice and structure are imaginative; **every factual claim** traces to an existing ownership-ledger event or a public cite already attached to that event. No invented prices, owners, dates, or events.

A *ghost franchise* is not a conspiracy. It is the afterimage a charter leaves when it changes city, name, league, or ownership grammar — and the ledger still knows which body is continuous.

## 1. The Sound That Left Town

Seattle still hears a name the league table no longer prints.

The SuperSonics were Sam Schulman’s expansion club — founding ownership from 1967, per the sale trail that later records him selling out. On 14 October 1983 the New York Times put the figure in daylight: Barry Ackerley bought the franchise for a reported $21 million (Ackerley’s own later accounting of cash-plus-deferred sometimes lands near an $11 million cash core; the ledger keeps both notes and invents neither). The Ackerley Group held until 11 January 2001, when an SEC exhibit announced the sale of the Sonics — and the WNBA Storm — to Howard Schultz’s Basketball Club of Seattle for $200 million.

In 2006 Professional Basketball Club LLC, led by Clay Bennett, bought the club for a reported $350 million. The ledger flags what came next without embroidery: in 2008 the franchise relocated Seattle → Oklahoma City and rebranded as the Thunder. Bennett’s group still controls as of the ledger’s 2026-09-19 as-of.

The ghost is not the Thunder’s roster. The ghost is the city that kept the nickname in its throat while the charter kept the games. Continuity of ownership through the move is the ledger’s quiet insistence: same corporate body, different skyline.

## 2. Four Cities Under One Net

Some franchises move once and settle. The Nets keep changing the stamp on the envelope.

In 1967 the ABA minted the New Jersey Americans under an Arthur J. Brown / Mark Binstein-led group — a franchise *award*, the ledger warns, not a secondary-market sale. By 1968 the club was on Long Island as the New York Nets. On 17 June 1976 it crossed into the NBA with Denver, Indiana, and San Antonio; the ABA–NBA merger entry charge is recorded at about $3.2 million — an entry fee, not a market price. Financial pressure after entry pushed the club back across the Hudson in 1977 as the New Jersey Nets.

The modern ghost story begins with a developer’s dream. On 22 January 2004 the New York Times reported Bruce Ratner / Forest City buying the Nets from YankeeNets for about $300 million — outbidding a Kushner/Corzine package near $267.5 million — still a New Jersey team, already pointed at Brooklyn. On 11 May 2010 the NBA Board of Governors approved Mikhail Prokhorov’s controlling stake (often cited near $200 million for control; enterprise packages vary); Ratner kept a minority through the arena path. In July 2012 New Jersey became Brooklyn at Barclays Center under continuous Prokhorov-era operators. Joe Tsai completed full ownership on 18 September 2019 (path often cited near $2.35 billion for full ownership; some packages nearer $3.4 billion including arena ops — the ledger records the ambiguity, invents none of it). In June 2024 the Koch family bought about 15% of BSE Global for $688 million at a reported $6 billion enterprise valuation; Tsai retained control.

Americans. Nets. New Jersey. Brooklyn. One event-id spine. The ghost is every prior letterhead still faintly visible under the current one.

## 3. Purple-and-Gold Inheritance

The Lakers are a chain of receipts with a championship logo stamped on each link.

Franchise origin, per the ledger: in 1947 Ben Berger and Morris Chalfen purchased the NBL Detroit Gems for a widely reported $15,000 and moved the club to Minneapolis as the Lakers. In 1960 Bob Short relocated Minneapolis → Los Angeles. In 1965 Jack Kent Cooke bought from Short for a reported $5.175 million — then a record NBA franchise price — and built The Forum. In 1979 Jerry Buss and partners bought a package from Cooke for about $67.5 million: Lakers + Kings + Forum + real estate (team-only price left intentionally blank). The Buss family controlled until a modern break in the chain.

On 30 October 2025 the NBA Board of Governors approved Mark Walter / TWG Global’s purchase of a majority interest at a $10 billion franchise valuation. Jeanie Buss remained governor; the Buss family retained a minority. As of the ledger’s 2026-09-19 access window, Walter is the controlling majority owner.

What haunts is not scandal — it is duration. Fifteen thousand dollars of NBL salvage becomes a ten-billion-dollar majority vote, and the jersey color scheme somehow survives every seller’s name on the bill of sale.

Sources: LAL-1947-sale; LAL-1960-relocation (https://www.spotrac.com/nba/ownership); LAL-1965-sale (https://www.sportingnews.com/us/nba/news/buss-family-timeline-lakers-history-ownership-jerry-jeanie/21e2539a8289edc39883bd59); LAL-1979-sale; LAL-2025-majority (https://www.nba.com/news/nba-approves-sale-majority-interest-lakers-mark-walter)

## 4. Four Survivors Walk Into the League

On 17 June 1976 four ABA clubs did not die. They paid an entry charge and put on NBA skins.

Denver, Indiana, the New York Nets, and San Antonio each carry a ledger row dated that day: ABA → NBA, entry economics about $3.2 million (plus, for some, pick forfeitures) — labeled `other`, not a market sale. Behind them sit founding ghosts: Denver’s path from a Kansas City award through Denver Larks to Ringsby Rocket Truck Lines renaming the Rockets (14 June 1967 rescue), later Nuggets; Indiana’s 1967 ABA founding under a local investor group; San Antonio’s Dallas/Texas Chaparrals origin (1967) and 1973 relocation into Spurs colors still inside the ABA; the Nets’ Americans/Nets trail already told above.

The ghost here is institutional. The ABA as a league evaporated; four charters did not. Writers who treat “ABA survivor” as nostalgia are half right — the other half is a fee, a BOG vote, and four continuous ownership spines that never needed resurrection because they never fully stopped.

## 5. The Hawks Who Never Nested

Ben Kerner’s Hawks were a traveling company before Atlanta was a destination.

Franchise origin: Buffalo Bisons (NBL, 1946) under Kerner, Leo Ferris, and B.W. Grafton — mid-season to Moline as the Tri-Cities Blackhawks. Relocations follow like stage marks: Tri-Cities → Milwaukee (1951); Milwaukee → St. Louis (1955), where the club won the 1958 NBA title under Kerner; then the sale. On 3 May 1968 Kerner sold the St. Louis Hawks to Thomas Cousins and Carl Sanders for a reported $3.5 million; the new owners relocated the franchise to Atlanta.

Later control chapters — Ted Turner’s January 1977 controlling (~55%) interest; Atlanta Spirit’s 2004 package (~$250 million era for Hawks + Thrashers + Philips Arena ops); Tony Ressler’s 2015 close at $850 million; a 2022 Dyal/Blue Owl minority — belong to Atlanta’s present. The ghost is the map: four cities before the skyline that now claims the bird.

Sources: ATL-1946-other (https://www.stargazette.com/story/news/local/2016/04/01/elmiras-leo-ferris-helped-shape-nba/82408904/); ATL-1951-relocation; ATL-1955-relocation; ATL-1968-sale; ATL-1977-majority (https://www.nytimes.com/1977/01/04/archives/turner-buys-the-hawks-day-after-baseball-ban.html); ATL-2004-sale (https://www.nba.com/hawks/news/atlanta_spirit_sale_040331.html); ATL-2015-sale (https://www.nba.com/hawks/news/group-led-tony-ressler-completes-purchase-atlanta-hawks)

## 6. The Name That Split in Two

Charlotte and New Orleans share a noun and dispute a ghost.

George Shinn’s expansion Hornets tipped off in 1988–89 (expansion fee recorded at $32.5 million). In 2002 that same owner moved operations to New Orleans — the franchise that would become the Hornets/Pelicans path. Charlotte returned via Bobcats expansion in 2004–05 under Robert L. Johnson ($300 million expansion fee). Michael Jordan took a majority stake in 2010 (reported $275 million). In 2014 the Bobcats rebranded as Hornets; the ledger’s crucial administrative ghost: Charlotte’s 1988–2002 history was reassigned to the current CHA franchise, while New Orleans’ franchise history is treated as starting 2002–03. New Orleans rebranded Hornets → Pelicans in 2013 under Tom Benson (who had bought from NBA stewardship in 2012 for a reported $338 million after the league’s December 2010 purchase from Shinn — the first league-owned franchise).

Two cities. One borrowed name. A 2014 paperwork séance that moved memory without moving a bus.

## 7. Clubs Without Franchises

EuroLeague’s ledger refuses the American sentence “who owns the team?” for half the room.

Real Madrid’s basketball section (founded 1931) sits under member-owned Real Madrid CF — `association_control`, not a private sale. FC Barcelona’s basketball section (official founding 24 August 1926) is likewise a member association multi-sport club. FC Bayern München e.V. founded its basketball section in 1946; in 2014 the pro side spun into a GmbH with the e.V. as sole shareholder — a corporate-form change, explicitly not a third-party sale. In Belgrade, KK Partizan (1945 within JSD Partizan) and KK Crvena zvezda (founded 4 March 1945 within SD Crvena zvezda) are sports-society member clubs: institutional/association models the methodology forbids dressing up as PE franchise transfers. Anadolu Efes (1976 Anadolu Group sports-club path) and Fenerbahçe’s basketball department (club continuity under Fenerbahçe SK) read the same grammar: multi-sport club control.

Elsewhere on the same EL sheet, private majority stories exist — Armani’s ~€4.09M path to Olimpia Milano (2008), Parker’s ASVEL majority (2014), Zanetti’s Virtus capital increase and later ~€5.8M buyback — which only sharpens the ghost: Europe runs two ownership languages in one competition. The American franchise is a transferable asset. The association club is a society with a basketball department. Confusing them is how invented sales get born.
